Michigan's governor's race began as one of the most competitive open-seat contests of the cycle, but Democrat Jocelyn Benson has emerged as a clear favorite over Republican John James, with recent polls showing a Democratic edge of roughly eight to ten points. Term-limited Gov. Gretchen Whitmer is leaving after eight years, and the race will decide whether Democrats win a third straight term in the governorship, something neither party has managed in roughly 35 years.

Michigan is still a genuine presidential battleground: Donald Trump narrowly carried it in 2024, the same ballot on which Democrat Elissa Slotkin won a Senate race. The race tests whether the coalition that elected Whitmer twice survives her departure, and whether Republicans can regain competitiveness in statewide Michigan elections.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jocelyn Benson | Democrat | Michigan secretary of state, elected 2018 and reelected 2022; former law school dean; nationally prominent for defending Michigan's election administration around the 2020 election; stresses accountability, affordability, public education, reproductive rights and economic development including the auto industry; running mate Winnie Brinks, the Democratic leader of the state Senate |
| John James | Republican | U.S. representative from suburban Detroit; Army veteran and former helicopter pilot and business executive; lost narrow U.S. Senate races in 2018 and 2020; stresses growth, manufacturing, lower taxes and public safety; Trump-endorsed but cultivating a business-oriented, suburban-friendly image; running mate state Rep. Jay DeBoyer |

Three minor-party candidates are also on the ballot, Douglas Campbell (Green), Anthony Hudson (Libertarian) and Donna Brandenburg (U.S. Taxpayers), none competitive in the polling.

## The Duggan withdrawal

One of the most consequential events was in May, when former Detroit Mayor Mike Duggan, a former Democrat, abandoned his independent campaign. He had tried to assemble a cross-party coalition of moderates, business interests and voters disillusioned with the two-party system, and for much of 2025 and early 2026 threatened to make the race an unpredictable three-way contest. On May 21 he withdrew, citing an increasingly polarized national environment and the difficulty of building an independent coalition amid dissatisfaction with Trump. Analysts generally saw it as helping Democrats, though some earlier polling suggested he drew more from Republicans. Without him the race became a conventional two-party contest.

## Michigan's geography

Michigan is one of the few states where urban, suburban, rural, industrial and union constituencies all matter statewide.

- **Detroit and Wayne County.** The largest Democratic vote reservoir. Benson needs strong turnout among Black voters and the wider Detroit metro. Arab American and Muslim communities in Dearborn and neighboring cities are also politically important after the foreign-policy disputes of 2024.
- **Oakland and Macomb counties.** Oakland's educated suburbs have moved substantially toward Democrats, while Macomb keeps a more working-class, Trump-friendly electorate. James represents part of Macomb, making it a test of his appeal.
- **Grand Rapids, Kent County and western Michigan.** Once a Republican stronghold, now more competitive as suburban voters shift. Kent is an indicator of whether Benson is winning beyond the traditional Democratic base.

Rural northern Michigan and much of the Upper Peninsula favor Republicans, and Saginaw, Genesee and other industrial counties are further tests of Democratic support among working-class voters.

## The policy differences

Michigan's next governor faces an economy both transforming and struggling with Rust Belt challenges: population stagnation, aging infrastructure, housing affordability and dependence on auto manufacturing.

| Issue | Benson (D) | James (R) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economy | Industrial diversification, workforce development, targeted investment | Tax cuts, deregulation, business expansion |
| Health care | Prescription-drug affordability board; protect Medicaid and ACA coverage | Price transparency, pharmacy-benefit-manager reform, consumer choice |
| Education | Public-school funding reform; $60,000 minimum teacher salary | School choice, parental oversight, federal education tax credits |
| Energy | Grid modernization, renewables, consumer protections | Reliability, lower energy costs, criticism of clean-energy mandates |
| Housing | Expand housing supply and affordability programs | Lower construction costs through regulatory reform |
| Data centers | Statewide moratorium on new development | Greater local authority to reject projects |

Two issues stand out. **The auto industry and Canadian trade:** Michigan's economy is deeply tied to Ontario's manufacturing supply chains, so tariffs can immediately affect production, investment and jobs. Benson attacks Trump's tariffs as damaging; James argues his relationship with the administration could help Michigan. **Data centers:** both candidates have taken restrictive positions on hyperscale development, though Benson's husband has worked with a data-center developer, creating a potential conflict-of-interest controversy, while James stresses local control. It shows how data-center opposition cuts across party lines.

## The Senate comparison

Michigan also has a highly competitive [Senate race](../../senate/mi/) between Democrat Abdul El-Sayed and Republican Mike Rogers. Recent polling generally has El-Sayed ahead, but Benson has often led James by more. Benson has won two statewide elections and El-Sayed comes from a more progressive tradition, while James has built name recognition through three previous statewide or federal campaigns. If Benson significantly outruns El-Sayed, it would suggest her experience, moderation and established reputation give her a candidate-specific edge beyond the national Democratic environment. The House races on the same ballot are [MI-4](../../house/mi-04/), [MI-7](../../house/mi-07/) and [MI-10](../../house/mi-10/).

## The legislature and 2028

Michigan has divided legislative control: Democrats hold a narrow state Senate majority and Republicans control the House. All state House seats and the regularly scheduled state Senate seats are up in 2026. A Benson win with Democratic legislative gains could restore unified Democratic government and allow more ambitious action on education, labor, energy and health care; if Republicans keep a chamber, she would have to negotiate with them, as Whitmer has during divided government. The governorship also matters for 2028 through appointments, election administration and certification, and Benson's background as secretary of state makes election administration a prominent part of her identity.

## Why it matters

- **Breaking the pattern.** A Benson win would end Michigan's long habit of alternating governors every eight years.
- **A strong Democratic hold in a battleground.** If she wins comfortably while Democrats also do well in the Senate and legislative races, Michigan could be one of the clearest examples of Democrats outperforming the party's recent presidential baseline.

## Possible results

- **Benson by 8–12 points:** Democrats' statewide coalition stays robust despite Trump's 2024 win, and Whitmer's legacy proves transferable.
- **Benson narrowly:** Michigan remains a highly competitive battleground, with Democrats helped by the national environment but not fundamentally changing the partisan balance.
- **James wins:** a significant Republican upset, ending eight years of Democratic governors and showing Republicans can win statewide in Michigan without Trump on the ballot.

## What to watch

Most Michigan polls close at 8 p.m. Eastern, with part of the western Upper Peninsula an hour later. Michigan allows advance processing of many absentee ballots, but reporting varies by county, so early results may not represent the final electorate. Watch Wayne, Oakland, Macomb and Kent counties and the gap between Benson and El-Sayed.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
