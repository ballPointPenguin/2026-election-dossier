Texas has emerged as one of the most consequential and unexpectedly competitive governor's races of 2026. Republican Gov. Greg Abbott faces Democratic state Rep. Gina Hinojosa in a contest that tightened dramatically over the fall. Abbott began the cycle an overwhelming favorite, but polling now has him only two to three points ahead, with two of the final surveys showing Hinojosa narrowly leading. That's remarkable for a state Donald Trump carried by about 14 points in 2024 and where Democrats haven't won a governor's race since 1990.

The race is also a referendum on Texas's defining controversies: school vouchers, property taxes, abortion, immigration enforcement, electricity infrastructure and the rapid spread of AI data centers.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Greg Abbott | Republican | Incumbent since 2015, 68; Texas attorney general for 12 years before that; seeking an unprecedented fourth consecutive term; has championed conservative social policy, school choice, border enforcement, tax restraint and aggressive business recruitment; built an extraordinarily well-funded political organization; strengths are incumbency, fundraising and Texas's Republican lean, while vulnerabilities include dissatisfaction with affordability and school funding |
| Gina Hinojosa | Democrat | Central Austin state representative since 2017; former labor attorney and Austin school board president; Brownsville native; combines economic populism with opposition to Abbott's education policies, stressing lower household costs, protecting public schools, health care and holding large corporations accountable; entered with little statewide recognition or money but became Abbott's most competitive challenger |
| Pat Dixon | Libertarian | Advocates limited government and individual liberty; polls around 1–2%, which could matter if the major-party race is very close |

The certified ballot confirms all three candidates.

## Texas's Republican dominance, and why 2026 differs

Texas hasn't elected a Democrat to statewide office since 1994; its last Democratic governor, Ann Richards, lost to George W. Bush that year. Abbott has won seven statewide elections, including three for governor, and his narrowest governor's win was still nearly 11 points. Texas's demographic change hasn't produced the straightforward Democratic realignment once expected: Dallas–Fort Worth, Houston, Austin and San Antonio have become increasingly important Democratic constituencies, but Republican gains among Hispanic voters, especially in South Texas, offset some of that, and Trump did particularly well along the Rio Grande in 2024. 2026 tests whether the newer Republican coalitions are durable or were tied to Trump and the conditions of 2024.

## Why Abbott is suddenly vulnerable

**1. School vouchers have caused a rural backlash.** Abbott's signature legislative achievement was a roughly $1 billion private-school voucher program, enacted after years of resistance from rural Republicans. Supporters say parents should have more choices; opponents say it diverts resources from public schools, especially in rural communities with few or no private alternatives. Hinojosa has made it a defining issue, portraying it as a subsidy for wealthier households. Rural Republicans have traditionally protected their local public schools, so a Democrat can criticize a conservative policy without leaning on national partisan arguments.

**2. Affordability has become the dominant economic issue.** Abbott's campaign first stressed property-tax cuts and widened its household-cost proposals as the race tightened. Hinojosa argues Texas's business-friendly model has produced growth without making everyday life affordable enough, and has proposed declaring a cost-of-living emergency, sending $1,500 payments to households and freezing electricity rates. Those proposals have appeal but would need legislative cooperation, funding and careful handling of electricity-market regulation, so they're campaign commitments, not actions a governor could necessarily impose alone.

**3. Data centers have become an unexpected populist issue.** Perhaps the campaign's most distinctive development. Texas has drawn huge interest from AI and hyperscale developers because of land, energy and incentives, but big projects have drawn opposition over electricity use, water, land and the risk that ordinary ratepayers bear infrastructure costs. Hinojosa seized on opposition in heavily Republican rural communities and called for a moratorium; Abbott first promoted the expansion as a major opportunity and later took a more restrictive position as resistance grew. It lets her cast herself as defending communities against politically connected corporations, a message that can cross party lines.

## The electoral geography

- **Houston and Harris County.** One of the country's largest Democratic vote reservoirs and a very diverse electorate: Hinojosa needs big turnout and strength among Hispanic, Black, Asian American and suburban voters.
- **Dallas–Fort Worth.** Dallas County is Democratic, while Tarrant County and the outer suburbs are more competitive, and the region's size makes modest suburban shifts consequential.
- **Austin and Central Texas.** Hinojosa's base: Travis County is overwhelmingly Democratic, but surrounding counties with growing suburbs and exurbs will help set the statewide margin.
- **San Antonio and South Texas.** A key test of Latino voting: Hinojosa, a Brownsville native, needs to recover Rio Grande Valley support while winning Bexar County comfortably.

West Texas, East Texas and much of the rural interior remain strongly Republican, so Abbott can lose the major cities by large margins and still win. One encouraging sign for Hinojosa is an October University of Texas survey in which she led among independent likely voters 67% to 17%; the same survey had Abbott ahead among rural voters 68% to 28%, showing the geographic challenge.

## The fundraising imbalance

Abbott's campaign is one of the most financially powerful gubernatorial operations in American politics. According to Election Day reporting, it spent about $117 million on advertising, more than four times Hinojosa's total, and it has a formidable donor network and years of infrastructure. Her competitiveness despite that suggests the national environment and her messages are doing a lot of work, but Abbott's resources still matter in a state with many expensive television markets.

## The Senate comparison

Texas also hosts one of the country's most important [Senate races](../../senate/tx/): Democratic state Rep. James Talarico against Republican Attorney General Ken Paxton, who beat Sen. John Cornyn in the primary. An October University of Houston survey had the governor's race at Abbott 49%, Hinojosa 46% and the Senate race at Talarico 48%, Paxton 46%. Talarico has generally polled slightly better than Hinojosa, and Paxton's controversial history creates different liabilities from Abbott's. That opens the possibility of Texas electing a Democratic senator for the first time in decades while keeping a Republican governor, or a strong Democratic turnout wave carrying both. The House races on the same ballot include [TX-15](../../house/tx-15/), [TX-28](../../house/tx-28/), [TX-34](../../house/tx-34/) and [TX-35](../../house/tx-35/).

## What a Democratic governor could actually do

Texas has a fragmented executive branch: the lieutenant governor, attorney general, comptroller and land commissioner are elected separately, and Republican Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick is especially powerful through his control of the state Senate's agenda. Republicans also control both legislative chambers, so a Hinojosa win wouldn't hand Democrats control of Texas policymaking. She could veto, direct agencies within their legal authority, make appointments and influence budget talks, but many of her most ambitious proposals would need a Republican legislature. Even so, ending Republican control of the governor's office would change the balance of power.

## Why it matters

- **A defining possible upset.** If Hinojosa wins, it would be one of 2026's defining upsets and could change the state's trajectory toward 2028; even a narrow Abbott win would suggest the electoral landscape is becoming more competitive.
- **Can the Republican coalition withstand a hostile national environment** against a Democratic campaign focused on economic issues, not traditional partisan identity?

The last two major polls both have Hinojosa narrowly ahead, though neither lead is statistically decisive. The polling margin isn't the same as the probability of winning: Abbott's two-point average edge, Texas's Republican lean and his resources still make him the favorite. For context, Abbott won in 2022 by nearly 11 points over Beto O'Rourke, so a two-point win would be a substantial deterioration in Republican statewide performance.

## Possible results

- **Abbott by 4–7 points:** Republicans keep their statewide advantage, but the narrowing suggests a meaningful Democratic resurgence.
- **Abbott by 1–3 points:** Texas stays Republican, but the race becomes one of the clearest demonstrations of the 2026 Democratic wave.
- **Hinojosa wins:** a historic upset ending more than three decades of Republican control of the governorship.

## What to watch

Texas polls generally close at 7 p.m. Central, 8 p.m. Eastern, with far West Texas on Mountain time. Early-voting turnout has been large, though that alone doesn't show which party benefits. Watch Democratic margins in Harris, Dallas, Travis and Bexar counties, Republican performance in Tarrant and the suburban counties, and whether Hinojosa recovers votes in South Texas.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
