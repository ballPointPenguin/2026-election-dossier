Ohio has become one of the more surprising governor's battlegrounds of 2026. Democrat Amy Acton, Ohio's former public-health director, faces Republican entrepreneur and former presidential candidate Vivek Ramaswamy to succeed term-limited Gov. Mike DeWine. Acton enters Election Day the favorite, ahead by about four points in polling averages, even though Ohio voted for Donald Trump by more than 11 in 2024.

That's a reversal of expectations: Ramaswamy began as the presumed favorite, with personal wealth, national name recognition and Trump's endorsement, but he has struggled to turn national conservative celebrity into a statewide advantage while Acton has run a surprisingly effective campaign. A Democratic win would end 16 straight years of Republican control of the governorship and make Acton Ohio's first woman governor.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amy Acton | Democrat | Public-health physician and former Ohio Department of Health director who became a household name with her widely watched briefings early in the COVID-19 pandemic under DeWine; political newcomer campaigning on health care, affordability, public education and a governor focused on ordinary households, not wealthy insiders; running mate David Pepper, former chair of the Ohio Democratic Party and former Cincinnati-area county commissioner |
| Vivek Ramaswamy | Republican | Biotechnology entrepreneur and former 2024 presidential candidate; advocates aggressive tax cuts, deregulation, school choice and restructuring government; closely tied to Trump and the national conservative movement; argues Ohio needs a successful entrepreneur to accelerate development and reform state government; running mate Rob McColley, president of the Ohio Senate |
| Don Kissick | Libertarian | Third-party candidate with running mate James L. Mills; polls around 2–4% in some surveys, potentially enough to matter in a close race, though his real support is uncertain |

## Ohio's realignment

Ohio was the quintessential presidential swing state, voting for the winner in every election from 1964 through 2016, until 2020, when it backed Trump while Biden won nationally. Its rightward shift resembles Iowa's, though Ohio keeps several large metro areas and a much bigger industrial economy. Republicans have dominated state government since 2011, DeWine won reelection in 2022 by about 25 points, and the last Democratic governor, Ted Strickland, served from 2007 to 2011. That makes Acton's polling lead striking: she's favored in a state that has become reliably Republican for president.

## The COVID-19 paradox

Acton's most unusual asset is also her biggest potential vulnerability. As DeWine's health director she became one of the state's most recognizable officials, and her calm briefings earned wide admiration. She also became a target of conservative opposition to lockdowns, public-health mandates and government restrictions. Ramaswamy has repeatedly attacked her pandemic record as having hurt businesses, schools and individual liberties, but the attacks haven't produced the expected backlash: many Ohioans remember her positively, and her service under a Republican governor reinforces her image as a professional, not a conventional partisan. The race tests whether voters have moved past the 2020 conflicts.

## The late campaign controversy

The final weeks were dominated by a deeply personal episode. Ramaswamy's campaign aired an advertisement featuring Acton's estranged mother, who attacked her daughter's character and urged voters not to support her. Acton has described a traumatic childhood, including sexual abuse by her stepfather, and has been estranged from her mother for decades. The ad drew condemnation across party lines: Republican Gov. DeWine criticized it, while Trump and Vice President JD Vance defended Ramaswamy. Acton's campaign reported a fundraising surge after it ran. It may have undermined Ramaswamy's attempt to appeal beyond the Republican base, since a campaign seen as excessively personal carries real risks when moderate Republicans and independents could decide the result.

## The policy differences

| --- | --- | --- |
| Taxes | Targeted affordability measures and maintaining public services | Aggressive tax cuts and a smaller state government |
| Health care | Improve access, affordability and preventive care | Competition, deregulation and market-oriented reforms |
| Education | Stronger public-school investment | School choice and educational competition |
| Economy | Workforce development, infrastructure and household affordability | Entrepreneurship, business investment and regulatory reform |
| Abortion | Supports reproductive rights | Socially conservative; supports abortion restrictions |
| Data centers | Greater safeguards for communities and infrastructure | Emphasizes investment and economic development |
| Government | Professional administration and bipartisan cooperation | Restructuring government and reducing bureaucracy |

It's an unusually sharp contrast, a public-health physician against a biotechnology entrepreneur, each presenting as an outsider to traditional electoral politics.

## Where the race will be decided

- **Cleveland and northeastern Ohio.** Cuyahoga County is a major Democratic stronghold: Acton needs strong turnout in Cleveland and its suburbs while limiting losses in surrounding former industrial Democratic communities.
- **Columbus and Franklin County.** The growing Columbus metro is increasingly important to Democratic statewide candidates: suburban voters, university communities and younger professionals are essential to Acton's coalition.
- **Cincinnati, Dayton and southwestern Ohio.** Traditionally more Republican, with significant Democratic urban constituencies and competitive suburbs. Ramaswamy needs to do well here.
- **Appalachian and rural Ohio.** Many formerly Democratic working-class communities have shifted decisively Republican. Ramaswamy needs big margins; Acton needs to limit the damage.

A mid-October On Point Polling survey had Acton leading independents 48–30 and winning about 12% of Trump voters, crossover that's central to her path.

## The Senate comparison

Ohio also has one of the nation's most important [Senate races](../../senate/oh/): former Democratic Sen. Sherrod Brown against appointed Republican Sen. Jon Husted. The October Marist poll had the governor's race at Acton 50%, Ramaswamy 43% and the Senate race at Brown 48%, Husted 44%. Both Democrats lead, but Acton's edge has generally been larger, which could mean Ramaswamy is personally less appealing than Husted, or that Acton's relatively nonpartisan reputation attracts voters less willing to back Brown. If both win, it would be an extraordinary reversal after Republicans' decisive 2024 victories; if Acton wins while Husted survives, it would show Ohio voters can still split tickets. The House races on the same ballot are [OH-7](../../house/oh-07/), [OH-9](../../house/oh-09/), [OH-10](../../house/oh-10/) and [OH-15](../../house/oh-15/).

## What an Acton victory would change

Ohio Republicans hold substantial majorities in both legislative chambers, so a Democratic governor wouldn't have the legislative freedom of one in Nevada or potentially Michigan. Acton could veto, appoint executive officials, direct agencies and negotiate budgets, but Republican supermajorities could potentially override vetoes. Ohio elects its secretary of state separately, so winning the governorship wouldn't give Democrats that office, though the governor influences policy, appointments and the institutional environment heading into 2028.

## Why it matters

- **A race that wasn't supposed to be competitive.** Ramaswamy has national prominence, resources and Republican establishment support; Acton has a distinctive public-service background, a comparatively moderate image and sustained independent support.
- **Not a swing-state restoration.** A Democratic win wouldn't make Ohio a presidential swing state again, but it would show a well-positioned Democrat can win statewide despite the rightward realignment.
- **A comparison with Iowa, Kansas and Nebraska,** other Republican-leaning states where Democrats are testing whether local credibility, moderation and a favorable national environment can overcome increasingly Republican electorates.

## Possible results

- **Acton by 4–7 points:** a remarkable Democratic pickup, suggesting Ohio's Republican dominance is less secure in a favorable national environment.
- **Acton narrowly:** candidate quality and Republican crossover voting prove decisive without necessarily signaling a durable realignment.
- **Ramaswamy wins:** Ohio's underlying Republican preference beats Acton's polling edge, raising questions about Democratic turnout and polling accuracy.

## What to watch

Ohio's polls close at 7:30 p.m. Eastern. Watch Cuyahoga, Franklin and Hamilton counties, the Appalachian and rural counties, and the gap between Acton's and Brown's results.
