Oregon is one of the more surprising governor's battlegrounds of 2026. Democratic Gov. Tina Kotek is seeking a second term against Republican state Sen. Christine Drazan, a rematch of their close 2022 race. Kotek is favored to survive, but the polling is essentially tied: recent surveys have them one to four points apart, even though Oregon voted for Kamala Harris by about 14 points in 2024.

That's an odd contrast with the rest of the country: Democrats are threatening Republican-held governorships in states like Ohio and Iowa while defending a surprisingly vulnerable incumbent in deep-blue Oregon. The explanation is largely local. Kotek has struggled with voter dissatisfaction over homelessness, housing affordability, taxes and a perception of ineffective state government, and Drazan has made those grievances the center of her campaign.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tina Kotek | Democrat | Incumbent, elected 2022; Oregon House speaker 2013–2022, one of the state's most experienced Democratic leaders; prioritizes homelessness, housing construction, behavioral health, education and reproductive rights; argues her administration has made progress on hard structural problems but needs more time; strengths are Oregon's Democratic electorate, labor support and governing experience; main vulnerability is that voters haven't consistently seen everyday conditions improve; also has the Working Families Party line |
| Christine Drazan | Republican | State senator from the Canby area south of Portland; former House Republican leader; the 2022 nominee who came within about 3.4 points of Kotek; stresses accountability, lower taxes and regulation, public safety, homelessness and a better climate for employers, presenting herself as a corrective to decades of Democratic control in Salem; her challenge is attracting independents and moderate Democrats without alienating Oregon's small but highly conservative Republican base; also has the Libertarian line |
| Brett Smith | Pacific Green Party | The only other candidate on the ballot; low single digits in polling, which could matter in a close race |

Oregon allows fusion voting, so candidates can hold nominations from more than one party.

## Oregon's Democratic gubernatorial streak

Oregon hasn't elected a Republican governor since Victor Atiyeh's reelection in 1982, and Democrats have held the office continuously since January 1987, an exceptionally long run of one-party control, even for a state that reliably votes Democratic for president. Its governor's races have often been closer than its presidential results:

| Election | Democratic margin |
| --- | --- |
| 2014 Governor | +5.8 |
| 2016 President | +11.0 |
| 2018 Governor | +6.4 |
| 2020 President | +16.1 |
| 2022 Governor | +3.4 |

Kotek's narrow 2022 win is especially relevant: she took about 47%, Drazan 43.5% and independent former state Sen. Betsy Johnson about 8.6%. Johnson isn't running in 2026, so the rematch is more directly Kotek against Drazan, but it would be simplistic to assume Johnson's former supporters will automatically vote Republican, since they included moderates, independents and voters unhappy with both parties.

## Why Kotek is vulnerable

**1. Homelessness and housing.** These were central promises of her first campaign. Her administration has invested heavily in shelter capacity, homelessness prevention and housing development, with tangible results including thousands of additional shelter beds and assistance that has prevented evictions. But the visible problem remains severe: federal counts show the number of Oregonians experiencing homelessness rose between 2023 and 2025, and her goal of 36,000 new homes a year remains far above current construction. She can point to substantial investment and new programs; Drazan can point to the continued severity of the problem. Both arguments have factual support.

**2. Taxes and economic dissatisfaction.** Oregon's economy has struggled with sluggish growth, high housing costs and concerns about business competitiveness. Drazan says excessive regulation and taxes have discouraged investment, particularly in Portland; Kotek says infrastructure, public services, housing and workforce investment are essential to long-term growth. A damaging episode was voter opposition to a transportation funding package involving higher taxes, which reinforced a sense that state leaders were asking residents to pay more without adequate services.

**3. Government competence and public confidence.** Kotek has been among the nation's least popular governors: an OPB poll found favorable views of her at about 38% against 45% for Drazan. Her administration has also faced controversy over the role of her spouse, Aimee Kotek Wilson, in state policy discussions and staffing decisions. Kotek stresses her policy expertise and administrative reforms, but the campaign has struggled to convince voters that Oregon's major problems are improving fast enough.

## The policy contrasts

| Issue | Kotek (D) | Drazan (R) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Housing | Public investment, homelessness prevention, expanded housing production | Land-use reform, deregulation, faster construction |
| Homelessness | Shelter expansion, behavioral-health services, prevention | Greater accountability, public safety, criticism of existing programs |
| Economy | Public investment, workforce development, infrastructure | Lower taxes, less regulation, business investment |
| Education | Public-school funding and expanded early childhood services | Greater accountability and changes to education governance |
| Abortion | Strongly supports reproductive rights | Conservative position; has opposed abortion-rights expansions |
| Immigration | Defends sanctuary protections | More receptive to federal enforcement priorities |
| Data centers | Supports temporary restrictions on new development | Also supports a moratorium |

Data centers are an area of convergence: both back a moratorium on new development over electricity demand, water use and public subsidies.

## The electoral geography

- **Portland and Multnomah County.** Oregon's Democratic center of gravity. Kotek needs big margins and strong progressive turnout, though homelessness, downtown conditions and dissatisfaction with city and state governance could shrink them.
- **Washington and Clackamas counties.** The Portland suburbs are crucial: Washington is strongly Democratic, Clackamas more competitive, and Drazan needs substantial crossover from suburban moderates to win statewide.
- **Eugene, Corvallis and the Willamette Valley.** University towns are reliable Democratic votes, while Salem and the surrounding valley hold more competitive suburban, agricultural and small-town constituencies.
- **Bend and Deschutes County.** One of Oregon's most interesting transitions: rapid growth and an influx of professionals have made Bend more Democratic even as surrounding central Oregon stays strongly Republican.

Southern and eastern Oregon are overwhelmingly Republican, with exceptions like Ashland. Drazan will win large geographic areas, but the question is whether she can get enough votes in the populous Willamette Valley.

## The national Republican problem

Drazan's best argument is that Oregon's Democratic establishment has failed to deliver effective government; her biggest liability is the national Republican Party. Kotek stresses the contrast between Oregon's progressive values and the Trump administration, particularly on abortion, LGBTQ+ rights, immigration and election administration, while Drazan tries to keep the focus on Oregon-specific problems. The Republican Governors Association has invested much less than in 2022 and Trump hasn't made the race a personal priority, and Kotek has a substantial fundraising edge. It's the classic tension: voters may be dissatisfied with Democratic governance in Salem yet unwilling to elect a Republican tied to Trump.

## What a Drazan victory would mean

Democrats control both chambers of the legislature and are unlikely to lose that entirely in 2026, so a Drazan win would probably mean divided government. She'd gain significant authority over executive agencies, appointments and budget priorities, and her veto could constrain Democratic legislation on taxes, labor, environmental regulation and social policy, but she'd have to negotiate with Democratic leaders for most of her agenda. A Kotek win would preserve the Democratic trifecta without resolving disagreements among progressives, labor and business-oriented moderates.

## Why it matters

- **A mirror image of Ohio and Iowa.** In those states Democrats are trying to win over Republicans unhappy with their state governments; in Oregon, Republicans are trying almost exactly the reverse.
- **The central question** is whether dissatisfaction with local Democratic governance can overcome resistance to the national Republican Party.

## Polling and ratings

Inside Elections downgraded Kotek from Lean Democratic to Tilt Democratic on October 29, a meaningful sign of her vulnerability. Kotek is favored largely because of Oregon's strong Democratic lean, not because she has built a convincing polling lead. A PolitiKool average on October 31, using polls weighted for recency, sample size and sponsorship, has Kotek at 45.3% and Drazan at 43.9%, a 1.4-point lead.

Oregon has had relatively little public polling in the final month, unlike Ohio or Michigan, so it's hard to tell whether the national environment has helped Kotek since early October. Polling alone suggests a toss-up, but most forecasts still favor Kotek because Oregon is so reliably Democratic.

## Possible results

- **Kotek by 5–8 points:** Oregon's Democratic partisan advantage ultimately outweighs dissatisfaction with the incumbent.
- **Kotek narrowly:** Democrats keep the governorship, but the result underscores the weakness of the state's political establishment and the continued salience of housing and homelessness.
- **Drazan wins:** one of 2026's most consequential Republican pickups, ending nearly four decades of Democratic gubernatorial control.

## What to watch

Oregon votes almost entirely by mail. Ballots must be at an official drop site by 8 p.m. Pacific, 11 p.m. Eastern, on November 3, or be postmarked by Election Day and received within the legal window, so a close race could take several days to resolve. Watch Multnomah, Washington, Clackamas and Deschutes counties.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
