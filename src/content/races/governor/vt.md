Vermont presents one of American politics' most extraordinary partisan paradoxes: the country's most Democratic presidential state has repeatedly elected a Republican governor by enormous margins. Gov. Phil Scott is seeking a sixth consecutive two-year term, and his challenger, progressive economist Amanda Janoo, has turned what looked like a hopeless Democratic campaign into Scott's most competitive reelection race in years.

Late polling is surprisingly close, including one survey giving Scott just a three-point edge and another showing Janoo ahead by six. Scott nonetheless remains the substantial favorite, because of his crossover appeal, polling that has historically underestimated him and a reputation as a moderate Republican, which makes Vermont a very different contest from states where Democrats are riding the national environment.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phil Scott | Republican | Incumbent, 68; first won in 2016 and has been reelected four times, most recently with about 73% in 2024; former lieutenant governor and construction-business executive and former stock-car racer; fiscal conservative with comparatively moderate social positions, supporting abortion rights, signing gun-control legislation and repeatedly criticizing Donald Trump; message is that Vermont needs fiscal restraint and political balance to counter the Democratic-dominated legislature |
| Amanda Janoo | Democratic / Progressive | Progressive economist, 39, and first-time candidate with an economic-development background who stresses the structural causes of Vermont's affordability crisis; platform includes universal primary health care, higher taxes on wealthy households and second homes, and more public investment in housing and essential services; running a distinctly progressive campaign rather than imitating Scott's centrism |

Three other candidates are on the ballot: Brian Judd (independent), June Goodband (Green Mountain Peace and Justice Party) and Dean Roy (Freedom and Unity Party), none expected to draw a substantial share.

## Vermont's ticket splitting

Vermont voted for Kamala Harris over Donald Trump by about 31 points in 2024, yet on the same ballot Scott beat his Democratic opponent by more than 51 points, receiving more than twice Trump's share of the statewide vote. He's kept that crossover coalition through personal popularity, social moderation, fiscal conservatism and a willingness to oppose Trump, and he even disclosed that he voted for Harris in 2024, which would be disqualifying for a Republican in many states but reinforces his independence here.

Vermont has a tradition of heterodoxy: Republican Sen. Jim Jeffords left the GOP in 2001, independent socialist Bernie Sanders has been in Congress since 1991, and the Vermont Progressive Party has a distinct presence. The electorate combines progressive social values, localism, environmentalism and a strong independent streak, so it isn't Democratic in the conventional national sense.

## Why Scott is more vulnerable in 2026

**1. Affordability and property taxes.** Vermont has an acute affordability problem relative to its modest wages and small economic base: housing is expensive, health costs are rising and property taxes are a major grievance. Scott says the state must constrain spending and expand its tax base; Janoo says the tax structure burdens working households while wealthier residents and second-home owners contribute too little. It's a substantive ideological disagreement, not just a personality contest.

**2. Education reform and local control.** A shrinking school-age population and high per-pupil spending have led Scott to propose consolidating districts and restructuring education governance to cut costs. Janoo opposes mandatory consolidation, arguing rural schools are community institutions and the state should address underlying cost drivers like health care and student support. The issue has weakened Scott in some rural communities where voters are fiscally conservative but protective of local schools, and a Vermont Public poll found broad opposition to reducing local control over school spending.

**3. Declining crossover support.** The September Vermont Public poll put Scott's approval at 45%, down from 60% in 2022, with approval among Democrats down 31 points, suggesting the national partisan environment is reaching even Vermont's unusually independent governor. Janoo may also be benefiting from a generational divide: younger, more progressive voters are skeptical of his economic policies even if they like his opposition to Trump.

## The candidates' economic philosophies

The October 21 Vermont Public debate showed a sharp contrast.

| Issue | Scott (R) | Janoo (D/P) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Taxes | Restrain spending, reduce taxes, expand the tax base | Raise taxes on high earners and certain property owners |
| Health care | Incremental insurance-market reforms | Universal publicly funded primary and preventive care |
| Education | Consolidation and structural cost reduction | Preserve local schools and expand community services |
| Housing | Increase supply through construction and regulatory reform | Address investor ownership, second homes and housing speculation |
| Energy | Open to small modular nuclear power | Skeptical of nuclear development and associated data-center expansion |
| Economic strategy | Population growth, business development, fiscal restraint | Redistribution, public investment and a wellbeing-oriented economy |

Janoo's health proposal is ambitious: she'd finance universal primary care with an income-tax surcharge on households earning more than $250,000. The appeal is guaranteed basic access, lower administrative costs and better preventive care; the difficulty is Vermont's small population and limited number of very high earners. Scott argues such a narrow tax base would be unreliable and could push wealthy residents to leave. That isn't merely theoretical: Vermont explored single-payer reform under Democratic Gov. Peter Shumlin and abandoned the financing plan in 2014 after concluding the tax increases needed were too large. Janoo's narrower primary-care plan differs from that universal-coverage effort, but the history shows the challenges of financing ambitious health reform in a small state.

**Housing.** Scott treats the crisis mainly as a supply shortage, arguing more homes would lower prices, attract younger workers, raise school enrollment and widen the tax base. Janoo stresses the financialization of housing, the conversion of homes into investments, vacation properties and short-term rentals, and would tax second homes and short-term rentals more to help permanent residents. Both mechanisms can matter: many communities have a genuine shortage while vacation demand can also distort local markets, and discouraging investment ownership doesn't automatically produce enough new housing, just as more building doesn't guarantee affordability for lower-income households.

## Political geography

- **Burlington and Chittenden County.** Vermont's most populous and progressive region: Janoo needs big margins, particularly among younger voters, university communities and progressive Democrats.
- **Montpelier and central Vermont.** Progressive communities alongside more moderate rural towns: opportunities for Janoo, though Scott keeps considerable personal support.
- **Rutland and southern Vermont.** A mix of working-class communities, tourism economies and progressive small towns, where affordability and local conditions may matter most.
- **Northeast Kingdom and rural Vermont.** Scott's moderate, pragmatic style has traditionally done very well; Janoo is trying to build support through opposition to school consolidation and concern about rural services.

## The legislature and the lieutenant governor

Vermont's legislature is overwhelmingly Democratic and progressive, though Republicans made significant gains in 2024, and Scott has often served as a veto-wielding counterweight. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each chamber, so how many seats Republicans win matters nearly as much as Scott's own reelection: if Democrats regain veto-proof supermajorities, Scott could remain governor while losing much of his power to block legislation, while enough Republican seats to sustain vetoes would keep his leverage. A Janoo win would pair a Democratic/Progressive governor with a Democratic legislature, potentially enabling big changes in taxation, health care and education.

Vermont also elects its lieutenant governor separately, and voters often split. Republican incumbent John Rodgers faces former Democratic Lt. Gov. Molly Gray, who has polled competitively; a Democratic win there alongside a Scott reelection would reinforce the state's split-ticket tradition.

## Why it matters

- **Perhaps the clearest candidate-specific appeal of 2026.** Scott is a Republican governor in a state that gave Harris more than 63% of its presidential vote, and has prospered by cultivating independence, moderation and administrative competence.
- **Janoo's argument** is that those qualities no longer suffice for Vermont's affordability problems, and her more ideological campaign still addresses housing, health care, rural schools and property taxes, issues that cross party lines.
- **A close Scott win would be politically significant; a Janoo win would be historic,** the first defeat of an incumbent Vermont governor in more than six decades.

A major complication: polls have systematically underestimated Scott. VTDigger's October analysis found that since 2016 his eventual vote share has averaged about 14 points higher than his pre-election polling. That doesn't mean he'll outperform by 14 points this time, only that Vermont polling has struggled to measure his crossover support. Forecasts consequently stay friendlier to Scott than the raw polls imply: Decision Desk HQ's October 30 forecast gave him a 93% chance with a projected margin around 20 points, though Sabato's Crystal Ball recently moved the contest toward greater competitiveness.

## Possible results

- **Scott by 15–25 points or more:** his personal coalition remains largely intact and late polls underestimated him again.
- **Scott by 3–10 points:** Janoo substantially eroded his crossover appeal even if she can't overcome his incumbency.
- **Janoo wins:** one of 2026's most remarkable upsets, ending Scott's long run as a check on the Democratic legislature.
