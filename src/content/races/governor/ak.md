Alaska is one of the more intriguing governor's races of 2026 and a genuine Democratic pickup chance in a state Donald Trump carried comfortably in 2024. Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins enters Election Day as the frontrunner, helped by a fragmented Republican field, an unusual electoral system and a strategic withdrawal by another prominent Democrat.

Republicans have held the governorship since 2018, but Mike Dunleavy is term-limited, so this is an open-seat contest, not a referendum on an incumbent. It also shows a distinctive feature of Alaska politics: Republican presidential voting doesn't automatically mean Republican loyalty in state elections.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins | Democrat | Former state representative from Sitka; relatively young, pragmatic, with experience representing rural and Southeast Alaska; stresses competent governance, public services and Alaska-specific concerns over national partisan fights |
| Dave Bronson | Republican | Former Anchorage mayor; conservative associated with the populist right; contentious tenure and lost his 2024 reelection; high name recognition and substantial baggage |
| Bernadette Wilson | Republican | Businesswoman and conservative activist; grassroots, anti-establishment; endorsed by Trump |
| Treg Taylor | Republican | Former Alaska attorney general under Dunleavy; more establishment-oriented; reached the general-election ballot after Democrat Tom Begich withdrew |

There are minor write-in candidacies too, but these four are the names to watch.

## The primary and the Begich withdrawal

In the August 18 top-four primary, the two leading Democrats together took roughly 43% of first-choice votes in a crowded field. Former Democratic state Sen. Tom Begich then withdrew, consolidating much of the Democratic opportunity behind Kreiss-Tomkins, and Taylor moved into the vacated ballot position. That may prove one of the most consequential strategic decisions of the election.

## The Murkowski endorsement

On October 27, Republican Sen. Lisa Murkowski endorsed Kreiss-Tomkins, and her father, former Sen. and Gov. Frank Murkowski, did too. It's an unusually significant cross-party endorsement, especially since Murkowski is also backing Republican Dan Sullivan for reelection in the [Senate race](../../senate/ak/). It signals that Kreiss-Tomkins appeals to Alaska's moderate, institutionally minded Republicans, not just a conventional Democratic coalition.

On the other side Trump has endorsed Wilson, and conservative organizations have urged their supporters to rank all three Republicans.

## Ranked-choice voting

Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, so three Republicans splitting first-choice votes doesn't automatically deliver a Democratic win: if Republican voters rank one another, their votes can consolidate through successive elimination rounds. The real question is whether Kreiss-Tomkins can get an outright majority or enough moderate-Republican second-choice support to beat the last Republican standing.

Alaskans are also voting on whether to repeal the top-four primary and ranked-choice general election system, so they're using it in a competitive governor's race while deciding whether to keep it.

## Why Alaska is distinctive

Alaska is conservative but not necessarily doctrinaire Republican. Its politics mix libertarian instincts, resource nationalism, heavy federal dependence, Indigenous political influence and a tradition of independent-minded politicians. Several issues make the governorship consequential:

- **Oil revenues and fiscal stability.** Petroleum production, the Permanent Fund Dividend and the state budget are perennial fights.
- **Rural Alaska.** Infrastructure, energy costs, health care, transportation and Indigenous sovereignty often matter more than national partisan controversies.
- **Education and public services.** School funding and rural service delivery are big questions given extraordinary logistical costs.
- **Institutional independence.** Alaska has a history of bipartisan legislative coalitions even when Republicans hold numerical majorities.

A Democratic governor wouldn't necessarily get a Democratic legislative majority. Kreiss-Tomkins could govern through cross-party coalitions with moderate Republicans and independents.

## Why it matters

- **A Democratic pickup in a Trump state.** The more interesting prospect is a relatively moderate Democrat assembling Democrats, independents, Indigenous voters and disaffected Republicans, with explicit support from one of the state's most prominent Republican families.
- **The Republican counterargument is mathematical:** ranked-choice transfers could consolidate the conservative vote efficiently.
- **The same ballot** carries the [Senate race](../../senate/ak/) between Mary Peltola and Dan Sullivan and the [House race](../../house/ak-al/) between Nick Begich III and Bill Hill.

## What to watch

Alaska's polls close at 8 p.m. local time, midnight Eastern. Counting takes a while, and a close contest may not be resolved tonight; the state is targeting November 25 for certification. Watch Kreiss-Tomkins's share of first-choice votes against 50%, how the Republican vote splits among Bronson, Wilson and Taylor, and how many Republican ballots rank him at all.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
