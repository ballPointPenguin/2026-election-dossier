Florida's governor's race is considerably more competitive than many observers expected a year ago, though Republicans remain favored to hold the office. Term-limited Republican Gov. Ron DeSantis is leaving after eight years, and the race pits Trump-endorsed Republican Rep. Byron Donalds against David Jolly, a former Republican congressman turned Democrat.

Donalds enters Election Day up roughly four to five points, but recent surveys range from a near tie to a large Republican lead. The broader question is whether Florida's dramatic rightward realignment is durable, or whether Democrats can begin rebuilding their once-formidable statewide coalition.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Byron Donalds | Republican | U.S. representative from Southwest Florida; Trump-endorsed frontrunner closely aligned with the MAGA movement; stresses immigration enforcement, lower taxes, school choice and continuity with DeSantis's direction; would be Florida's first Black governor; running mate state Sen. Bryan Avila |
| David Jolly | Democrat | Former U.S. representative for the Tampa Bay area as a Republican (2014–2017); became a prominent Trump critic, left the GOP and joined the Democrats in 2025; centrist reformer stressing affordability, homeowners insurance, housing costs, public education and moving past DeSantis-era culture wars; running mate Gwen Graham |

Libertarian Scott Jewett and independent Frank Russo are also on the ballot, neither polling close to the major-party candidates.

## Florida's transformation

Florida was once the quintessential swing state and is now substantially more Republican than the country.

| Election | Republican margin |
| --- | --- |
| President, 2016 | R+1.2 |
| Governor, 2018 | R+0.4 |
| President, 2020 | R+3.4 |
| Governor, 2022 | R+19.4 |
| President, 2024 | R+13.1 |

In 2018 DeSantis beat Democrat Andrew Gillum by barely 32,000 votes; four years later he won by about 1.5 million. The shift reflects Republican gains among Hispanic voters, an influx of conservative-leaning migrants, growth of conservative retirement communities and the decay of the Florida Democratic Party's organizing infrastructure. Republicans now hold a voter-registration edge of roughly 1.5 million, a striking reversal of the Democrats' historical advantage. For Jolly to win, he needs a favorable national environment and a big outperformance of the party's recent Florida baseline.

## Where the race could be decided

- **Miami-Dade County.** Once a source of enormous Democratic margins, it voted decisively Republican in 2024, with Cuban Americans moving especially strongly toward the GOP. A Democratic recovery here would be an important sign the realignment is weakening.
- **Tampa Bay and Pinellas County.** Jolly's former congressional turf. His name recognition and moderate Republican past could help with suburban independents and crossover voters around St. Petersburg and Tampa.
- **Orlando and Central Florida.** Orange and Osceola counties hold large Democratic and Puerto Rican constituencies, and turnout and margins here are essential to any statewide win.
- **Southwest Florida and retirement communities.** A major part of today's Republican electorate, and Donalds's home base. Modest Democratic gains among older and suburban voters could matter, but Republicans keep a formidable structural advantage.

## The issues

The most potent Democratic argument may be economic, not cultural. Florida's housing and insurance markets have become unusually expensive, especially on the coasts: high property-insurance premiums, hurricane exposure, condominium assessments and rising property taxes. Jolly makes affordability central, trying to shift attention from DeSantis-era cultural fights to household finances. Donalds stresses tax relief, deregulation and the state's economic growth, presenting himself as the natural successor.

A University of North Florida poll found Jolly narrowly ahead on trust on affordability, 43% to 42%, while Donalds led on property insurance, 43% to 38%, so the economic argument is competitive but not automatically a Democratic advantage.

## The running mates

Donalds chose Republican state Sen. Bryan Avila. Jolly chose Gwen Graham, a former Democratic congresswoman, daughter of the late Florida Gov. and Sen. Bob Graham, and a figure in the state's moderate Democratic tradition. Graham's selection reinforces Jolly's pitch to voters nostalgic for Florida's more bipartisan era.

## Why it matters nationally

- **A long drought.** Florida hasn't elected a Democrat to a full governor's term since Lawton Chiles in 1994. A Jolly win would be an extraordinary reversal, particularly after Trump's 13-point win two years ago, and a setback for the idea that Florida is permanently Republican.
- **A Donalds win** would consolidate the state's MAGA-era transformation and could make him a nationally prominent Republican governor.
- **The legislature** is expected to stay Republican, though Democrats are trying to break the GOP supermajorities, so a Democratic governor would likely govern through vetoes, negotiation and executive authority.
- **The same ballot** carries the [Senate race](../../senate/fl/) between Ashley Moody and Angie Nixon and House races including [FL-7](../../house/fl-07/), [FL-9](../../house/fl-09/), [FL-13](../../house/fl-13/), [FL-14](../../house/fl-14/), [FL-22](../../house/fl-22/), [FL-25](../../house/fl-25/) and [FL-27](../../house/fl-27/).

Stetson's October 1–9 survey had Donalds up 11, and its final October 26–30 survey had him up six, evidence of tightening, though two surveys alone can't establish a trend.

## Three possible outcomes

- **Donalds by 6–10 points:** Florida stays firmly Republican, with only modest Democratic recovery.
- **Donalds by 1–5 points:** Republicans keep control but Florida is meaningfully more competitive, suggesting Democrats could contest it again.
- **Jolly wins:** a major upset, implying an exceptionally strong national environment or a dramatic Florida-specific shift.

A useful benchmark is the national environment: if Democrats win the House popular vote by about nine points, as some late projections suggest, even a narrow Republican win in Florida would show a state well to the right of the nation.

## What to watch

Florida polls close at 7 p.m. Eastern, and at 8 p.m. Eastern in the Central-time western Panhandle. Florida reports quickly. Watch Miami-Dade, the Tampa Bay suburbs, Orlando-area turnout and the Southwest Florida retirement counties.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
