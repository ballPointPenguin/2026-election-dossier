Nevada has one of the closest governor's races in the country, pitting Republican incumbent Joe Lombardo against Democratic Attorney General Aaron Ford. There's no clear polling favorite: recent surveys range from a narrow Lombardo lead to a modest Ford edge, and the final Noble Predictive Insights poll has them tied at 47%.

The stakes are large. Democrats already control both legislative chambers, so beating Lombardo would restore unified Democratic state government and could unlock legislation on housing, health care, labor rights and education that Lombardo has repeatedly vetoed. Nevada also narrowly voted for Donald Trump in 2024, making this a test of whether Republican gains among working-class and Latino voters are lasting.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Joe Lombardo | Republican | Incumbent, elected 2022; 34-year law-enforcement career including eight years as sheriff of Las Vegas and Clark County; business-oriented conservative stressing public safety, school choice, tax restraint and economic development; reputation for pragmatism and occasional work with Democrats on housing and infrastructure; liabilities include the national Republican environment, his Trump ties and a big veto record |
| Aaron Ford | Democrat | Attorney general since 2019; former teacher, attorney and state Senate majority leader; has won two statewide elections; stresses affordability, consumer protection, public education, prescription-drug costs and working-class opportunity, and argues Lombardo has obstructed bills that would make life cheaper; would be Nevada's first Black governor |
| Danielle Ford | Nonpartisan | State Board of Education member and former Clark County school trustee, running without a party; no relation to Aaron Ford; support has swung a lot in polling, making her a possible minor spoiler in a very tight race |

All three are on the ballot, along with Nevada's "None of These Candidates" option, which cannot win.

## Nevada's history: a real battleground

For much of the 2000s and 2010s Democrats built a formidable operation around Las Vegas's hospitality industry and organized labor, and the infrastructure tied to the late-era Harry Reid helped them win close statewide races even in bad national years. Nevada has moved right recently, particularly among Latino and working-class voters. Two results stand out:

- In 2022 Lombardo beat Democratic Gov. Steve Sisolak by about 1.5 points while Sen. Catherine Cortez Masto narrowly survived.
- In 2024 Trump became the first Republican presidential candidate to carry Nevada since George W. Bush in 2004, yet Sen. Jacky Rosen won reelection on the same ballot.

Nevada voters clearly split tickets, so extrapolating from Trump's win isn't a safe guide to this race.

## Geography

- **Clark County (Las Vegas and Henderson).** About three-quarters of Nevada's population lives in the Las Vegas area. Ford needs a big margin, especially among Latino, Black, union and younger voters, though Lombardo's long career as Clark County sheriff gives him unusually strong local recognition.
- **Washoe County (Reno and Sparks).** The most important swing county. Reno's growing tech and professional workforce has made it more competitive for Democrats, while surrounding suburbs and exurbs lean Republican.
- **Rural Nevada.** Overwhelmingly Republican but sparsely populated; Lombardo needs commanding margins to offset Ford's expected edge in Clark.

Perhaps the most important wild card is unaffiliated voters, nearly 40% of the electorate, partly a result of automatic voter registration. In the final Noble poll Ford led independents 45–39 with 10% undecided, and there was a sharp generational split: voters under 45 favored Ford by 24 points, older voters favored Lombardo by 15.

## The policy debate

Both candidates see the economy as the dominant issue. An Emerson poll found 43% of Nevada voters naming the economy as their top concern, followed by housing affordability at 15%.

| Issue | Lombardo (R) | Ford (D) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Housing | Expand construction and access to developable federal land | Restrict large corporate home purchases and strengthen affordability protections |
| Education | More funding, school choice and accountability | Greater public-school investment and free school meals |
| Health care | Recruit health-care professionals and keep a business-friendly environment | Prescription-drug cost controls and consumer protections |
| Labor | Preserve Nevada's right-to-work framework | Repeal right-to-work and expand worker protections |
| Public safety | Law enforcement and tougher criminal-justice policy | His record as attorney general and consumer protection |
| Data centers | Development incentives with economic benefits | Tighter restrictions and scrutiny of water and energy use |

**Labor.** Nevada is a right-to-work state, yet Las Vegas has one of the country's most politically influential private-sector labor movements, especially the Culinary Workers Union. Ford explicitly supports repealing right-to-work, an ambitious position for a statewide Democrat. If Democrats win the governorship and keep the legislature, repeal becomes a real possibility, a major union victory that would face fierce business opposition and isn't guaranteed to pass.

**Tourism and tariffs.** Nevada's reliance on hospitality makes it sensitive to consumer confidence, international travel and discretionary spending. Las Vegas has seen a tourism slowdown, including a reported 17% drop in Canadian visitors in 2025 amid U.S.–Canada trade tensions. Ford says Trump's tariffs and economic policy contributed; Lombardo points to his investments in housing and education and warns Ford's proposals could make Nevada less attractive to employers.

**Data centers.** Nevada is becoming a hub for hyperscale data centers, especially around Reno and on or near federal land, raising questions about water, electricity demand, public subsidies and whether the benefits justify the costs. An Emerson survey found 59% of Nevada voters opposed data centers in or near their communities against 31% in favor, with opposition across parties but strongest among Democrats and independents. Ford wants restrictions; Lombardo stresses development and investment.

## Vetoes and the trifecta

Lombardo's biggest institutional role has been checking the Democratic legislature. He has vetoed more bills than any previous Nevada governor, 162 over two legislative sessions, according to recent reporting, including measures on prescription-drug prices, paid family leave, housing ownership and school meals. Ford's campaign argues many of those policies have legislative support and would become law with a Democratic governor. That makes Nevada different from Kansas, where a Democratic governor would still face a heavily Republican legislature. The legislature ordinarily meets for only 120 days every two years, so the 2027 session is an unusually important window.

## Controversies and endorsements

The campaign has turned increasingly negative. Lombardo's allies have attacked Ford over a clemency application he supported for a convicted killer and over sponsored international travel while attorney general; an ethics complaint about the travel has advanced, which is not a finding of wrongdoing. Ford criticizes Lombardo's ties to wealthy donors and developers, his veto record and his relationship with Trump. Lombardo got a crossover endorsement from Clark County's Democratic district attorney, notable given both men's law-enforcement backgrounds. California Gov. Gavin Newsom and former Vice President Kamala Harris have campaigned for Ford.

## Why it matters

- **The only thing between Democrats and a trifecta.** The governorship is the one office standing in the way of full Democratic control of state government.
- **A signal for 2028.** Nevada's young, diverse, working-class and fluid electorate will be scrutinized for clues about the next presidential race.
- **House race on the same ballot:** [NV-2](../../house/nv-02/).

## Possible results

- **Ford by 2–4 points:** Democrats show the 2024 Republican gains were reversible and gain unified state government.
- **Ford narrowly:** a strong Democratic midterm environment proves just enough to beat a relatively popular Republican incumbent.
- **Lombardo wins:** Nevada keeps splitting tickets, choosing a Republican governor as a check on Democratic legislative power.

## What to watch

Nevada's polls close at 7 p.m. Pacific, 10 p.m. Eastern. The result may not be known on election night: Nevada accepts mail ballots arriving up to four days after the election if postmarked by Election Day, a small share of the total but possibly decisive in a race this tight. Watch Clark and Washoe counties, the unaffiliated vote and the generational split.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
