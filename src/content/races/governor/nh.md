New Hampshire is one of the more intriguing cases of ticket splitting in the 2026 midterms. Republican Gov. Kelly Ayotte is favored for reelection even as Democrats look well placed in the state's congressional and U.S. Senate races. Her challenger, Democrat Cinde Warmington, has made affordability and opposition to the Trump administration central.

Ayotte has led nearly every autumn poll, generally by six to eleven points, but a final University of New Hampshire poll released November 1 showed the two tied at 48%, adding real uncertainty to what otherwise looks like a fairly secure Republican hold. New Hampshire voted for Kamala Harris by about 2.8 points in 2024, making it one of the few Democratic presidential states with a Republican governor seeking reelection.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kelly Ayotte | Republican | Incumbent, 58; former state attorney general and U.S. senator (2011–2017); won the governorship in 2024 and seeks a second two-year term; pragmatic, fiscally conservative, stressing housing construction, public safety, education, low taxes and energy affordability; has sometimes broken with the Republican legislature, vetoing bills her own party backed, which helps with moderates and unaffiliated voters |
| Cinde Warmington | Democrat | Former executive councilor (2021–2025) and health-care attorney, 69; lost the 2024 Democratic gubernatorial primary and won the nomination unopposed this year; stresses household affordability, health care, housing and opposition to Trump's immigration and economic policies; her main liability is her past lobbying for Purdue Pharma, which Ayotte's campaign keeps raising and Warmington has tried to explain and defend |
| Stephen Villee | Libertarian | Qualified by petition; usually polls around 2–3%, which could matter in a close race; his party also needs enough votes to restore recognized ballot status |

Ayotte won about 92% of the September Republican primary, and Warmington had no Democratic primary opponent.

## New Hampshire's ticket splitting

New Hampshire's political culture is socially moderate, fiscally cautious, skeptical of broad taxes and protective of local government. It has voted Democratic for president since 2004, but Republicans have held the governorship since 2017.

The state is comfortable electing a Democratic president or senator alongside a Republican governor, as Ayotte's predecessor Chris Sununu showed in four wins. An October AARP survey had Ayotte ahead of Warmington 52–45 while Democrat Chris Pappas led his Republican opponent in the Senate race; among unaffiliated voters, a particularly large share of the electorate, Ayotte held a modest five-point edge. That's Warmington's challenge: she can't simply count on disapproval of Trump, but has to persuade voters that Ayotte herself deserves to lose.

## The major policy disputes

| Issue | Ayotte (R) | Warmington (D) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Housing | Expand supply through permitting and zoning reform | Housing coordinator, more state intervention |
| Education | Expanded Education Freedom Accounts and school choice | Reverse voucher expansion, protect public-school funding |
| Health care | Defends her budget and its fiscal restraints | Reverse new Medicaid premiums and higher drug copays |
| Cannabis | Opposes recreational legalization | Supports legalization |
| Immigration | Supports stronger enforcement | Opposes state cooperation with ICE |
| Energy | Supports new generation, including small modular nuclear reactors | Focuses on affordability and consumer costs |
| Data centers | Supports a moratorium through the next budget | Promises an immediate moratorium |

Housing is especially important: southern New Hampshire is tied to the greater Boston economy, and rising property values, limited construction and high rents are major concerns. Both candidates acknowledge it but differ on how aggressively the state should intervene. On data centers, public opposition has become intense enough that both now favor temporarily restricting new projects.

## The governor's institutional constraints

New Hampshire and Vermont are the only states that elect governors every two years, so Ayotte is seeking reelection after two years in office. The state also has a five-member, separately elected Executive Council that must approve many appointments and big state contracts, so the governor lacks the unilateral appointment power found elsewhere. The legislature is unusually large, with a 400-member House and a 24-member Senate. Republicans control both chambers and four of five Executive Council seats, and Democrats hope for legislative gains in 2026, which could produce divided government even if Ayotte wins.

Ayotte has been willing to veto Republican-sponsored bills, which lets her campaign as a check on her own party, not an extension of the legislature.

## The Senate race

The open [Senate race](../../senate/nh/) between Democrat Chris Pappas and Republican John E. Sununu may be the biggest outside factor. Pappas has led recent polls, including 54–42 in the final UNH survey that found Ayotte and Warmington tied, and UNH's reading was that Democratic enthusiasm from the Senate contest could produce down-ballot support for Warmington. That's plausible, but it runs into New Hampshire's record of ticket splitting: Pappas winning comfortably while Ayotte holds the governorship would fit the state's history.

## Why it matters

- **A clear test of ticket splitting.** If Ayotte survives comfortably while Democrats sweep the federal contests, it will be one of 2026's clearest examples.
- **Does the state distinguish the national party from a Republican governor seen as independent?** That's the central question.
- **Warmington's best path** is a large Democratic turnout wave driven by the Senate and congressional races, plus dissatisfaction over housing, health-care costs and education policy.

## Possible results

- **Ayotte by 6–10 points:** the traditional New Hampshire pattern persists, with moderate Republican governors viable despite Democratic strength in federal races.
- **Ayotte narrowly:** Democratic turnout and national conditions have weakened but not eliminated the Republican gubernatorial advantage.
- **Warmington wins:** a significant Democratic pickup and evidence the 2026 environment overcame even New Hampshire's strong tradition of gubernatorial ticket splitting.
