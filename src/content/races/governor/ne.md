Nebraska's governor's race has become unexpectedly competitive in 2026, though Republican Gov. Jim Pillen is still the clear favorite for a second term. His main challenger is former Democratic state Sen. Lynne Walz, who has made affordability, public education and Pillen's record central. Two other candidates are also on the ballot, including a former Republican state senator, in a state Donald Trump carried by about 20.5 points in 2024.

Recent polling puts Pillen roughly six points ahead, much closer than Nebraska's partisan makeup would suggest.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jim Pillen | Republican | Incumbent, elected 2022; former veterinarian and agribusiness executive and University of Nebraska regent; has pursued property-tax cuts, conservative social policy, abortion restrictions and education reforms; running mate Lt. Gov. Joe Kelly |
| Lynne Walz | Democrat | Former state senator for Fremont and surrounding areas in Nebraska's officially nonpartisan legislature (2017–2025); former teacher and real-estate professional; stresses property-tax relief, school funding, health-care access and bipartisan governance; running mate Ben Steffen |
| Brett Lindstrom | America First Party | Former Omaha-area Republican state senator who unsuccessfully sought the 2022 GOP nomination; left the party and qualified under the America First label, though his candidacy isn't an extension of Trump's movement; a more moderate, business-oriented history may let him draw disaffected Republicans; running mate Michael Picard |
| Rick Beard | Legal Marijuana NOW | Omaha wine professional and private chef representing the marijuana-reform movement; running mate Keenya Barnes |

All four tickets are on the general-election ballot.

## Nebraska's Republican history

Nebraska is one of the most consistently Republican states: Republicans have held the governorship since 1999, and the last Democrat to win was Ben Nelson, reelected in 1994. Unlike Kansas, which has repeatedly elected Democratic governors despite its Republican presidential lean, Nebraska shows relatively little ticket splitting in governor's races, so a Walz win would be a much bigger historical upset than a Democratic win in Kansas.

## What made Pillen vulnerable

- **Fiscal strain.** Revenue pressure and budget shortfalls as earlier tax cuts take effect and federal funding changes hit state programs. Pillen says Nebraska must control spending rather than reverse tax cuts; Walz says it needs more sustainable budgeting and better protection of essential services.
- **Property taxes.** Rural landowners and suburban homeowners alike are frustrated with assessments and bills. Pillen has made relief a central goal, but comprehensive reform has met legislative resistance.
- **Weak personal popularity.** An October National Public Affairs survey had his favorability at 41% to 53% unfavorable and showed him keeping only 73% of registered Republicans in the governor's race, warning signs for an incumbent in a heavily Republican state.

## Policy differences

| Issue | Pillen (R) | Walz (D) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Property taxes | Reduce the burden through restructuring and spending restraint | Tax relief alongside more sustainable school and local-government funding |
| State budget | Continue tax cuts and constrain spending | Protect services and reassess fiscal priorities |
| Education | School choice and conservative education policy | Stronger public-school funding |
| Medicaid | Work requirements and tighter eligibility administration | Greater emphasis on access and affordability |
| Abortion | Supports restrictions | Supports reproductive rights |
| Economic development | Business incentives, agriculture and tax competitiveness | Workforce development, infrastructure and small-business support |

Nebraska's public-power system, agricultural economy and emerging data-center industry also raise questions about electricity costs, infrastructure investment and whether development incentives pay off for residents.

## The unicameral legislature

Nebraska has the only unicameral state legislature in the country, and it's officially nonpartisan: its 49 senators run without party labels, though affiliations and coalitions are well understood. There are no partisan chambers to flip, and coalitions vary by issue. It takes 33 votes to overcome a filibuster, a supermajority threshold that can constrain a governor even when most senators share her partisan preferences. A Walz victory wouldn't automatically yield a Democratic governing coalition; she would need moderate Republicans and independents, as in her legislative career.

## The geography of a possible upset

- **Omaha and Douglas County.** The biggest metro area and the most important Democratic vote reservoir. Walz needs large margins here plus suburban independents and moderate Republicans.
- **Lincoln and Lancaster County.** A university and government employment center increasingly favorable to Democrats, where strong turnout is essential.
- **Fremont and smaller eastern cities.** Walz's home territory, where she must outperform recent Democrats in places Republicans usually dominate statewide.
- **Rural Nebraska.** The Republican foundation. Pillen's agricultural background helps, though property taxes and farm-economy pressures could let Walz trim Republican margins.

## The Dan Osborn factor

The [Senate race](../../senate/ne/) between Republican Pete Ricketts and independent Dan Osborn, a former union leader who nearly beat Deb Fischer in 2024, is arguably more competitive than the governor's race, and it's built on working-class populism and independence from both parties. That creates several possibilities: Osborn could win while Pillen is reelected, showing the strength of candidate-specific independent populism; Walz could benefit from higher turnout among independents and anti-establishment voters Osborn attracts; or a broader anti-incumbent wave could threaten both Pillen and Ricketts, though that is the less likely governor outcome. Omaha's 2nd District also has a competitive congressional race, another reason to watch Douglas and Sarpy counties.

## Spending

The Republican and Democratic Governors Associations have invested in a contest that would normally get little national attention. Pillen and Walz together had raised nearly $20 million, with more than $17 million spent. Walz has raised more than any previous Democratic gubernatorial candidate in Nebraska, though Pillen keeps a substantial financial edge. Trump and Vice President JD Vance have supported Pillen, and Kentucky Gov. Andy Beshear has campaigned for Walz.

## Why it matters

- **Not a toss-up, no longer routine.** Pillen has incumbency, more money and a powerful structural advantage; Walz has a credible campaign, a potentially favorable national environment and evidence that many Nebraskans are dissatisfied with the incumbent.
- **Compared with Iowa and Kansas:** all three are heavily Republican Midwestern or Plains states with unusually competitive governor's races, but Nebraska is the steepest climb for Democrats because it lacks Kansas's tradition of electing Democratic governors and Iowa's strong candidate-specific Democratic polling edge.
- **A narrow Pillen win would still be a significant Democratic overperformance; a Walz win would be an earthquake.**

## Possible results

- **Pillen by 10–15 points:** Nebraska stays reliably Republican despite some dissatisfaction and an unfavorable national environment.
- **Pillen by 3–8 points:** a substantial Democratic overperformance, suggesting Nebraska is more competitive than its presidential results indicate.
- **Walz wins:** one of 2026's most remarkable Democratic upsets, ending nearly three decades of Republican control.

## What to watch

Nebraska's polls close at 8 p.m. Central and 7 p.m. Mountain, 9 p.m. Eastern. Early and absentee ballots may be in counties' first totals, so the initial margin may shift. Watch Douglas and Lancaster counties, Walz's Fremont area and the gap between Pillen's and Ricketts's results.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
