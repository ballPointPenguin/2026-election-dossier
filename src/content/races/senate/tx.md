Texas has become one of the most extraordinary Senate battlegrounds of 2026. Republican Attorney General Ken Paxton faces Democratic state Rep. James Talarico in a race that could end more than three decades of Republican dominance in statewide Texas elections.

It combines unusual circumstances: a Republican senator beaten in his own primary, a controversial nominee closely aligned with Trump, a young Democrat using explicitly Christian language for progressive economic arguments, and a statewide electorate more competitive than expected. Talarico enters Election Day with a modest but consistent polling edge, yet Texas voted for Trump by about 14 points in 2024, so a Democratic win would be one of the most dramatic reversals of the midterms.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| James Talarico | Democrat | State representative from the Austin area; former public-school teacher and Presbyterian seminarian; backs public education, abortion rights, affordable health care and curbing billionaire and corporate influence; blends economic populism with progressive Christianity |
| Ken Paxton | Republican | Texas attorney general since 2015; leading MAGA figure known for litigation against Democratic administrations and conservative positions on immigration and abortion; career marked by significant legal and ethics controversies |

Libertarian Ted Brown is the third printed-ballot candidate, and four certified write-ins are also running.

## How Texas became competitive

The Republican primary may be the single most important event of the race. Four-term Sen. John Cornyn, first elected in 2002, was seeking another term; reliably conservative, he had occasionally broken with Trump, notably on bipartisan gun legislation. Paxton challenged him from the right as an insufficiently loyal establishment figure. The fight was extraordinarily expensive and bitter, and Republican Senate leaders worried Paxton's controversies would make the general election needlessly competitive. Trump endorsed Paxton shortly before the May runoff, which helped him beat Cornyn.

The result was an inversion: Republicans nominated the candidate most closely tied to Trump, but perhaps least able to win the independents and moderates needed in a hard midterm.

## Paxton's controversies

The Republican-controlled Texas House impeached Paxton in 2023 on corruption-related charges, and the state Senate acquitted him. He has also faced securities-fraud allegations and federal investigations, none of which produced a criminal conviction, and his wife's divorce filing alleging infidelity became another campaign issue. Paxton denies wrongdoing and calls the controversies politically motivated. Talarico makes them central to his argument that Texas politics is dominated by powerful insiders without enough accountability.

## What makes Talarico unusual

Talarico is progressive on public education, abortion rights, inequality and many social issues, but his presentation differs from the national party's usual language. As a Presbyterian seminarian he often invokes Christian theology: obligations to the poor, welcoming outsiders and resisting wealth's corrupting influence. That contrasts with Paxton's Christian-conservative politics: both speak to religious voters with very different readings of what Christianity requires politically.

He also separates economic populism from cultural liberalism, arguing working Texans have more in common with each other than with the wealthy donors financing campaigns. His vulnerabilities: Republicans highlight his past comments about gender, religion and cultural issues to portray him as more progressive than Texas will accept, and Talarico has acknowledged some earlier remarks were poorly phrased.

## Geography

Texas's demographic change is enormous, but rapid urban growth hasn't produced a straightforward Democratic realignment.

## The Latino vote

One of the biggest developments of 2024 was Republican improvement among Latino voters, especially in South Texas, where Trump made major gains in traditionally Democratic border counties and complicated the assumption that Texas's growing Latino population would eventually deliver the state to Democrats. In 2026, immigration enforcement, economic dissatisfaction and the candidates' appeals have made those voters competitive again.

An October University of Texas poll found Talarico ahead 54% to 41% among Latino likely voters and 54% to 44% among suburban voters, while Paxton led rural voters 66% to 30%. That suggests two Democratic paths: recovering Latino support lost in 2024 and persuading suburban Republicans unhappy with Paxton.

Cumulative candidate and associated-committee fundraising through October 14, as reported by The Texas Tribune. Talarico raised $64 million in the third quarter alone, a Senate record for that period. The outside-spending story is even bigger: Elon Musk contributed $158 million to the Republican Senate Leadership Fund in September, with large sums later directed to ads backing Paxton or attacking Talarico, and other Republican groups also invested heavily. Combined advertising commitments topped $500 million by late October, putting Texas on course to be the most expensive Senate race ever.

That gives Talarico a rhetorical argument about billionaire influence, though Democratic-aligned groups also get substantial support from wealthy donors.

## Why it matters nationally

- **Historic.** Texas last elected a Democratic senator in 1988 (Lloyd Bentsen) and a Democrat to any statewide office in 1994, so a generation of Texans has never seen a Democratic statewide win.
- **The warning from 2018.** Beto O'Rourke lost to Ted Cruz by only 2.6 points, but Cruz won in 2024 by about 8.5, a reminder of how hard it's been to turn demographic change and fundraising into statewide wins.
- **A new route to the majority.** With Republicans defending competitive seats in Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, Alaska, Iowa, Kansas and elsewhere, a Texas pickup would give Democrats another path to the net four seats they need. It would also change perceptions of the electoral map.
- **The governor's race as a comparison.** Republican Gov. Greg Abbott, seeking a fourth term, faces Democratic state Rep. Gina Hinojosa, and the October UT poll had Hinojosa at 49% to Abbott's 48% against Talarico's 50% to 47% over Paxton. If Talarico wins while Abbott survives, Paxton's liabilities may have been decisive; if both Democrats win, a broader realignment or backlash is under way.

## What to watch

Texas polls close at 7 p.m. local time, 8 p.m. Eastern, with far western counties on Mountain Time. Early voting was extraordinary, about 7.7 million ballots before Election Day, above previous Texas midterm totals. High turnout doesn't say who benefits, but it shows the intensity.

Watch Harris County turnout, Talarico's showing in the Dallas–Fort Worth suburbs, whether Democrats recover ground in the Rio Grande Valley, and how many traditional Republicans split their tickets or leave the Senate line blank.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
