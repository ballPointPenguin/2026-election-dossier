Michigan is one of the most important open-seat Senate contests of 2026. Sen. Gary Peters's retirement leaves a closely watched race in a state Donald Trump narrowly won in 2024. Democrats are defending a seat they've held continuously since 1979, and Republicans see a chance to break a long losing streak in Michigan Senate races.

The state packs in many of the forces shaping the midterms: industrial jobs, organized labor, manufacturing tariffs, suburban realignment, Arab American disaffection from the Democratic Party, and a contest for working-class voters.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abdul El-Sayed | Democrat | Physician and epidemiologist; former Detroit health director; progressive commentator; ran for governor in 2018; backs Medicare for All, economic populism, stronger labor protections and campaign-finance reform |
| Mike Rogers | Republican | Former FBI agent; congressman 2001–2015 and House Intelligence chair; stresses national security, border enforcement, manufacturing and conservative economics; narrowly lost the 2024 Senate race |

Other ballot-qualified candidates are Lydia Christensen (Libertarian), Douglas Marsh (Green), Timothy Long (U.S. Taxpayers) and Walter Kristy (Natural Law). None is competitive for first place, though minor-party votes could matter in a close finish.

## The Democratic primary

El-Sayed beat two prominent rivals: Rep. Haley Stevens, associated with the party's pragmatic, business-friendly wing, and state Sen. Mallory McMorrow, who became nationally known for fighting Republican culture-war politics. He was the progressive alternative, backed by Bernie Sanders's movement and stressing economic redistribution, universal health care and a more confrontational stance toward corporations. That was a major win for the Democratic left in a state that isn't safely Democratic. The primary also exposed divisions over Israel and Gaza and the influence of pro-Israel organizations.

The most remarkable late development came on November 1, when Stevens endorsed Republican Mike Rogers over her own party's nominee, two days before the election. It raises the question of whether El-Sayed can unite enough moderates and traditional Democrats, even in a favorable national climate.

## Michigan's geography

Michigan is a microcosm of the changing Democratic coalition: overwhelmingly Democratic cities, competitive suburbs, union-influenced industrial towns, socially conservative working-class voters and increasingly Republican rural counties.

## The issues

- **Manufacturing and populism.** Both candidates court voters worried about industrial jobs, autos, trade, inflation and wages. El-Sayed wants expanded health coverage, stronger unions and lower household costs through more government intervention; Rogers stresses domestic manufacturing, energy, tax cuts and deregulation, and ties himself to Trump's economic nationalism. Both are appealing to economically insecure voters who distrust institutions.
- **Israel, Gaza and Arab American voters.** The most distinctive issue here. El-Sayed, who is of Egyptian descent and Muslim, has sharply criticized Israel's military operations in Gaza and U.S. support for them, which has helped mobilize Arab American and progressive voters disillusioned with the Biden administration's foreign policy. Rogers strongly supports Israel and attacks El-Sayed's positions. The campaign has featured accusations of Islamophobic rhetoric and disputes involving pro-Israel groups: AIPAC-affiliated groups spent heavily against El-Sayed in the primary and have supported efforts benefiting Rogers in the general election, per Al Jazeera. El-Sayed would be the first Muslim U.S. senator. The arithmetic is complicated: he may win back Arab American voters who left in 2024 while losing some Jewish voters and moderate Democrats.
- **The Iran war.** El-Sayed criticizes U.S. involvement and says resources should go to domestic priorities. Rogers backed Trump's intervention while also calling for it to end, and his national-security credentials give him a different profile from many Republicans.

## Money

Michigan's Senate race has drawn nearly $300 million in spending, one of the most expensive of 2026, from national party organizations, Trump-aligned groups, major Democratic donors and pro-Israel organizations.

## Why it matters nationally

- **An indispensable defensive seat.** If Republicans win it, Democrats must flip another Republican-held seat to compensate.
- **A test of the left.** An El-Sayed win would show that a Medicare-for-All, Israel-critical, openly progressive candidate can win statewide in a battleground. A Rogers win would suggest that internal disagreements over ideology and foreign policy can overcome a favorable national climate.
- **Compare it with the other Michigan races.** If Democratic candidates for governor substantially outrun El-Sayed, that would point to candidate-specific weakness, not a general Republican resurgence. The House races on the same ballot are [MI-4](../../house/mi-04/), [MI-7](../../house/mi-07/) and [MI-10](../../house/mi-10/).

## What to watch

Michigan polls close at 8 p.m. Eastern in most of the state, with a small western part of the Upper Peninsula on Central Time, and a close result could take until Wednesday. Watch Detroit turnout and Democratic margins, El-Sayed's performance in Dearborn and other Arab American communities, and whether Rogers can repeat his 2024 strength among Macomb County's working-class voters. Strong Democratic showings in Oakland and Kent could make up for weaker margins elsewhere.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
