New Hampshire is an important Democratic defensive seat. Sen. Jeanne Shaheen is retiring after three terms, leaving an open seat in a state that has voted Democratic in every presidential election since 2004 but regularly elects Republican governors. The state is fiscally conservative, socially libertarian, independent-minded and used to ticket-splitting, with no general sales tax or broad-based income tax, and its electorate is less predictably partisan than its presidential record suggests.

For Republicans it's one of their better theoretical chances to take a Democratic Senate seat; for Democrats, holding it matters to any realistic path to the majority.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chris Pappas | Democrat | Representative from the competitive 1st District (Manchester and much of the Seacoast) since 2018; comparatively moderate; family restaurant background; stresses affordability, health care, veterans and opposition to Trump administration policies |
| John E. Sununu | Republican | Senator 2003–2009 after six years in the House; fiscal conservative from one of New Hampshire's best-known political families; trying a comeback after losing to Shaheen in 2008 |
| Edmond Laplante | Constitution Party | On the ballot |

Sununu won the Republican primary over former Massachusetts Sen. Scott Brown, who had lost a New Hampshire Senate race in 2014; Trump endorsed Sununu despite his more traditional Republican identity. Pappas beat progressive challenger Karishma Manzur with about 65% of the Democratic primary vote, a win for the party's pragmatic, establishment wing.

## The Sununu dynasty and an unusual rematch

John E. Sununu's father, John H. Sununu, was governor from 1983 to 1989 and then White House chief of staff under George H. W. Bush, and his younger brother Chris Sununu was governor from 2017 to 2025. John E. won the Senate seat in 2002 by defeating then-Gov. Shaheen, and she beat him in the 2008 rematch. Eighteen years later he's running to succeed the senator who ended his Senate career.

A small campaign detail: Sununu's signs prominently feature his surname, sometimes without his first name, and Democrats say that capitalizes on his brother's popularity. Sununu rejects the idea that voters can't tell them apart.

The state has voted Democratic for president six times running, by widely varying margins, and Republican governors have stayed successful. That contrast is vivid in 2026: Republican Gov. Kelly Ayotte leads Democrat Cinde Warmington in several polls while Pappas leads Sununu. An October AARP survey had Pappas ahead 51–44 and Ayotte ahead 52–45; among undeclared voters, roughly 40% of the electorate, Pappas led by about 20 points and Ayotte by five. It's a clear case of real ticket-splitting, not a state drifting from one party to the other.

## The issues

- **Affordability and housing.** Housing costs are high and both candidates stress lowering household expenses. Pappas says Trump's tariffs and economic policies made life more expensive; Sununu blames Democratic spending and regulation for inflation and favors lower taxes and less government intervention.
- **Social Security.** Pappas criticizes Sununu's past support for exploring privatization. Sununu says his earlier proposals aimed to strengthen the program's finances and that he opposes cutting benefits. It's especially salient with older voters.
- **Trump.** Sununu tries to keep distance from Trump despite his primary endorsement; Pappas tries to nationalize the race, arguing a Sununu win would strengthen Trump's Senate majority.
- **Experience.** Both have substantial Washington experience. Sununu portrays Pappas as an ineffective career politician, and Pappas says Sununu's decades-old Washington ties are the kind of politics voters want to move past.

The campaign has also featured aggressive negative ads about Sununu's business relationships and connections and Republican attacks on Pappas's voting record. Some accusations have been contested or lack enough evidence to support their more sensational implications.

Outside spending helped close the gap: by November 1, independent groups had reported about $63 million in general-election spending, roughly $39 million benefiting Sununu and $24 million benefiting Pappas. That's a remarkable amount for a state of about 1.4 million people and explains the advertising saturation locals describe.

## Why it matters nationally

- **A Democratic defense.** It's one of four Democratic-held seats with no incumbent running in 2026, and Democrats must hold seats like it while flipping several Republican ones to take the chamber.
- **Historic if Pappas wins.** He would be the first openly gay man elected to the Senate; Tammy Baldwin became the first openly gay senator in 2012.
- **A Sununu win** would restore a prominent dynasty to the Senate and show Republicans can still win federal races in a Democratic-leaning New England state with a moderate, locally established candidate.

## What to watch

New Hampshire polls close between 7 and 8 p.m. Eastern depending on the municipality, and results come from hundreds of local jurisdictions, so statewide totals can develop unevenly. Watch Pappas's margins in Manchester and Nashua, Democratic performance in the Seacoast and Upper Valley, and the gap between the Senate and gubernatorial results. If Ayotte wins comfortably while Sununu loses, it would reinforce that New Hampshire voters separate their state and federal preferences.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
