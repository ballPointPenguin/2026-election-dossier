Ohio is one of the most consequential and genuinely competitive Senate races of 2026: a comeback attempt by former Democratic Sen. Sherrod Brown against Republican Sen. Jon Husted, appointed to fill JD Vance's seat when Vance became vice president.

What makes it remarkable is that Ohio has moved decisively right, yet Brown enters Election Day with a narrow polling edge. Trump carried Ohio by about 11 points in 2024, the same election in which Brown lost his seat to Republican Bernie Moreno. Two years later Brown is trying to return by beating a different Republican senator.

It's also a special election: the winner serves the remaining two years of Vance's original term, with another election in 2028.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sherrod Brown | Democrat | Senator 2007–2025; earlier a congressman and Ohio secretary of state; economic populist, labor advocate and trade skeptic, known for stressing the dignity of work |
| Jon Husted | Republican | Appointed incumbent (by Gov. Mike DeWine, January 2025); former lieutenant governor, secretary of state and Ohio House speaker; conventional pro-business Republican who has largely backed Trump's legislative agenda |

The ballot also includes Libertarian Bill Redpath, independent Gregory Levy and qualified write-ins.

## Ohio's shift

Ohio was the quintessential presidential bellwether, voting for the winner in every election from 1964 through 2016. That ended in 2020, when Trump carried Ohio while Biden won nationally. The realignment came mostly from falling Democratic support among white working-class voters, particularly in eastern and northeastern Ohio.

| Region | Significance |
| --- | --- |
| Cleveland / Cuyahoga County | Major Democratic stronghold; Black turnout and suburban margins critical |
| Columbus / Franklin County | Fast-growing, educated, increasingly Democratic |
| Cincinnati / Hamilton County | Democratic-trending urban and suburban electorate |
| Akron / Summit County | Historically Democratic industrial territory, still competitive |
| Toledo / Lucas County | Union and manufacturing base important to Brown |
| Youngstown / Mahoning County | Former Democratic stronghold that has shifted sharply Republican |
| Dayton / Montgomery County | Competitive urban-suburban region |
| Appalachian southeast and rural western Ohio | Strong Republican territory; Brown must limit his losses |

Brown's old formula combined big metro margins with crossover support in industrial communities. Whether it still works is the central question.

## Brown's comeback

| Election | Result |
| --- | --- |

| 2012 Senate | Reelected by about 6 |
| 2018 Senate | Reelected by about 7 |
| 2024 Senate | Loses to Bernie Moreno by about 3.5 |
| 2026 Senate | Comeback attempt against Husted |

His 2018 win was especially impressive since Ohio had already voted decisively for Trump in 2016. But 2024 suggested even his working-class appeal might not overcome the realignment. There's a historical symmetry: Brown entered the Senate by beating DeWine, who is now governor and appointed Husted to the seat Brown wants back.

## The issues

- **Data centers, electricity prices and the AI boom.** Perhaps the most unusual issue in any major Senate race this year. Ohio has become a big data-center hub, especially around Columbus, promising construction jobs, taxes and investment but provoking intense opposition over electricity use, utility bills, water and land. A September Bowling Green State University survey found 75% opposed a data center in their own community and 63% opposed more construction anywhere in Ohio. Brown criticizes tax incentives granted during Husted's time as lieutenant governor and says developers should pay the full infrastructure costs of their electricity demand. Husted backs legislation to stop large electricity users from shifting costs onto households; his Ratepayer Protection Act passed the House overwhelmingly but didn't advance in the Senate, and Democrats said its requirements weren't binding enough. Construction unions benefit from the boom while many households and rural communities oppose it, so Brown has kept major union endorsements while criticizing parts of it, and Husted has also won some construction-union support. It may preview a national split over AI infrastructure and who gets its benefits.
- **Manufacturing, unions and trade.** Brown is one of the party's best-known economic populists, long opposed to trade deals he thinks hurt manufacturing workers, a supporter of collective bargaining and industrial policy, and skeptical of free trade since before Trump. Husted favors lower taxes, deregulation, domestic investment and Trump's economic agenda, including tariffs. They disagree partly on how to promote American industry, not whether to.
- **Trump, inflation and the Iran war.** Husted has largely backed Trump's agenda, including the big Republican tax-and-spending law, tariffs and Iran policy; Brown ties these to higher household costs. In Marist's October poll Trump had 43% approval and 55% disapproval among likely Ohio voters, and 44% said their family finances had worsened over the past year. That's an opening Brown didn't have in 2024, when Trump himself was on the ballot.
- **Ethics and the establishment.** Brown and allies attack Husted over ties to the state's political and business establishment, including the nuclear-plant bailout associated with the House Bill 6 bribery scandal. Husted hasn't been accused of criminal wrongdoing in that investigation. There have also been disputes over donations from billionaire Les Wexner, and Husted says he gave the available contributions to an anti-trafficking charity. Husted portrays Brown as a career Washington politician too liberal for Ohio.

## Money

Reported advertising spending and reservations through late October top $500 million combined, which would make Ohio the second-most expensive Senate race of the cycle and one of the costliest nonpresidential races in American history, in a state considered safely Republican two years ago.

## Why it matters nationally

- **Possibly needed.** Republicans began with 53 seats to Democrats' 47 counting two caucusing independents, so Democrats need a net gain of four to overcome the vice-presidential tiebreaker. Because it's a special election, a Brown win would add a Democratic seat as soon as the new senator is seated.
- **What a Brown win says.** The national environment would have shifted enough to overcome a large Republican structural edge, and a labor-oriented populist message still competes in the industrial Midwest. A Husted win would reinforce Ohio's realignment even in a hard national year.
- **The governor's race is another test.** Democrat Amy Acton faces Republican Vivek Ramaswamy, and Marist had Acton up seven to Brown's four. If both Democrats win, it suggests a broad backlash; if Acton wins and Brown loses, candidate-specific factors may be decisive.
- **House races on the same ballot:** [OH-7](../../house/oh-07/), [OH-9](../../house/oh-09/), [OH-10](../../house/oh-10/) and [OH-15](../../house/oh-15/) will help show whether Ohio is seeing a broader Democratic resurgence.

## What to watch

Ohio polls close at 7:30 p.m. Eastern. Counties generally report most early and absentee ballots first, so early results may lean Democratic before Election Day votes are counted. Per the source, an automatic statewide recount is triggered at a margin of 0.25% or less. Watch Brown in Mahoning and Trumbull counties, the industrial areas around Toledo and Akron, and the suburbs around Columbus and Cincinnati, which will show whether he rebuilt his crossover coalition or is relying mostly on metropolitan turnout.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
