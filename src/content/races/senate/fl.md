Florida's Senate race is a special election to finish the term of Marco Rubio, who resigned in January 2025 to become Donald Trump's secretary of state. Gov. Ron DeSantis appointed Attorney General Ashley Moody to the vacancy, and she's now asking voters for the remainder of the term, through January 2029.

It matters nationally because Democrats would almost certainly need an upset in a Republican-leaning state like this one to assemble a majority. But Florida has moved so far right over the past decade that this is a much harder pickup than Alaska, Maine or North Carolina.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ashley Moody | Republican | Incumbent; Florida attorney general 2019–2025; former prosecutor and judge; closely aligned with DeSantis and Trump on immigration, public safety and judicial policy |
| Angie Nixon | Democrat | Challenger; Jacksonville state representative since 2020; progressive focused on affordability, health care, housing and inequality; would be Florida's first Black U.S. senator |
| Neil Gillespie | No party affiliation | Little attention; not expected to win |

## The Democratic primary

Nixon's nomination was a mild surprise. She beat Alex Vindman, the retired Army lieutenant colonel known for his testimony in Trump's first impeachment, about 56% to 44%. Vindman had more national name recognition and a more moderate profile; Nixon won with a grassroots, progressive campaign. That sets up the strategic question for Florida Democrats: a moderate who might win back disaffected Republicans, or a progressive trying to mobilize voters the state party has lost touch with? Nixon is the second, though she also stresses bread-and-butter economics.

Florida Democrats haven't won a statewide race since 2018, when Nikki Fried narrowly took agriculture commissioner. The shift has several causes:

- **Miami-Dade:** once a Democratic stronghold, it has moved sharply right, especially among Cuban American and other Latino voters.
- **Migration and retirees:** Republican-leaning retirees and domestic migrants have changed the electorate, though migration isn't uniformly Republican.
- **Organization:** Republicans built a formidable registration and turnout operation while the state Democratic Party struggled with organization, fundraising and recruiting.
- **Working-class realignment:** Republicans gained among some noncollege and Hispanic voters.
- **Polarization:** DeSantis and Trump made Florida a center of conservative identity, which attracts like-minded voters and sharpens sorting.

The Associated Press says Republicans now have about 1.5 million more registered voters than Democrats. That structural deficit is arguably more consequential than any single poll.

## The issues

Cost of living dominates: fast-rising housing costs, homeowners insurance and electricity bills. Nixon campaigns on higher wages, health care, housing and corporate power, with proposals including a $25 federal minimum wage, Medicare for All and stronger housing-market intervention. Moody calls those socialist and economically destructive, and stresses tax cuts, deregulation, immigration enforcement and Trump's legislative agenda.

An interesting wrinkle: a University of North Florida survey found 61% of likely voters prefer prioritizing environmental protection over economic growth, against 26% for growth, and even a plurality of Trump voters sided with the environment. Both candidates talk about electricity costs and data-center development; Nixon wants a moratorium on new data centers.

## Why it matters nationally

- **A reach, not a target.** Florida is a seat Democrats hope for rather than count on.
- **Is the realignment reversible?** A Nixon win would show Florida's Republican shift can reverse under favorable national conditions; a comfortable Moody win would suggest it's structural.
- **2028 again.** The winner faces voters in 2028, when Rubio's original term would have ended.
- **A progressive test.** Can a populist economic message compete where Democrats keep losing?
- **House seats on the same ballot.** [FL-13](../../house/fl-13/), [FL-14](../../house/fl-14/), [FL-22](../../house/fl-22/), [FL-25](../../house/fl-25/) and [FL-27](../../house/fl-27/) are among the House races to watch alongside it.

A Democratic-sponsored RedBox Research poll released November 2 had Moody up only 47–45, notably tighter than the rest. A polling-based forecast updated November 2 projects Moody winning by about 5.7 points with an 82% chance, one model's estimate, not a consensus probability.

Moody is the clear favorite, but Nixon has made it closer than Florida's recent presidential margins would suggest. An upset would take a large polling miss or unusually strong Democratic turnout.

## What to watch

Florida reports quickly. Polls close at 7 p.m. Eastern in most of the state and 8 p.m. Eastern in the Central-time western Panhandle. Watch whether Democrats recover ground in Miami-Dade, run up margins in Broward and Palm Beach, do well in the Tampa Bay suburbs, and lift turnout around Jacksonville and Orlando.

A Moody win by five or six points fits the polls and would still be a big Democratic improvement on 2024. A one- or two-point win would suggest a much larger wave, and a Nixon victory would be a major national upset.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
