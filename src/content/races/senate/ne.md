Nebraska features a rematch of sorts: Republican Sen. Pete Ricketts against independent Dan Osborn, who nearly beat Republican Sen. Deb Fischer in 2024 despite Nebraska's overwhelming Republican presidential preference. Now he's trying to finish the job against Ricketts, a wealthy former governor appointed to the Senate in 2023.

Osborn isn't running as a Democrat. His campaign rests on working-class economic populism, labor organizing and independence from both parties, which has made a normally safe Republican state a genuine battleground.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dan Osborn | Independent | Former industrial mechanic, Navy veteran and union leader who helped organize the 2021 Kellogg's strike in Omaha; backs higher wages, stronger labor protections, congressional term limits and less corporate influence |
| Pete Ricketts | Republican | Incumbent; governor 2015–2023; businessman from the family behind TD Ameritrade and the Chicago Cubs; appointed in 2023, elected in the 2024 special election, now seeking his first full term |

The ballot also includes Chuck Conboy (America First), Mike Marvin (Legal Marijuana NOW) and Robin Richards (Nebraska Working People). Democratic nominee Cindy Burbank withdrew in July, leaving Osborn as the main alternative to Ricketts.

## Why Osborn is unusual

His breakthrough was the 2021 Kellogg's strike, a 77-day labor dispute at the Omaha cereal plant, where he became a prominent organizer and later lost his job. In 2024 he challenged Fischer with no Democrat on the ballot and lost by only about seven points in a state Trump won by roughly twenty. His appeal combines things rarely found together in a Senate campaign:

- a working-class biography rooted in manufacturing and organized labor;
- populism aimed at corporate consolidation and wealthy donors;
- skepticism of both major parties and the establishment;
- a culturally moderate presentation that avoids some of the national Democratic Party's more polarizing issues;
- a focus on Nebraska's agricultural and industrial interests.

He's trying to assemble Democrats, independents, union households and Republicans unhappy with the party's economic policies.

## Independent isn't Democratic

Osborn says he won't caucus with either major party if elected, which separates him from independents like Bernie Sanders and Angus King, who caucus with Democrats. Republicans argue his Democratic financial support shows he'd effectively serve Democrats; Osborn rejects that. An Osborn win would remove a Republican senator without automatically adding a Democrat, though his choices on committees, Senate organization and the majority-leader vote could be consequential in a closely divided chamber.

## Geography

| Region | What matters |
| --- | --- |
| Omaha / Douglas County | Osborn's home base and the state's largest urban electorate |
| Lincoln / Lancaster County | University, government and professional voters, increasingly Democratic |
| Bellevue / Sarpy County | Suburban and military communities, potentially decisive |
| Grand Island / Hall County | Agricultural and manufacturing economy, traditionally Republican |
| Scottsbluff and western Nebraska | Deeply Republican; Osborn needs to limit losses |
| Rural eastern Nebraska | Conservative, but hit by agricultural trade policy |

Nebraska splits its presidential electors by congressional district, and Omaha's 2nd District has voted Democratic at times, including in 2020 and 2024. That gives Osborn a foundation, but he can't win on urban votes alone and needs real support in smaller towns and rural areas.

## The issues

- **Agriculture, tariffs and affordability.** Trump's tariffs, high fuel prices and uncertain farm markets hurt farmers, ranchers and manufacturers. Osborn attacks both parties for failing working people and says corporate concentration squeezes producers and consumers. Ricketts defends the administration's economic approach and stresses tax policy, deregulation and farm legislation. Both highlighted a new federal farm bill in their final appearances.
- **The billionaire and the mechanic.** Ricketts comes from one of Nebraska's wealthiest families, and his father founded TD Ameritrade. Osborn spent nearly two decades as an industrial mechanic. Osborn frames the race as wealthy insiders against working Nebraskans; Ricketts answers that his executive experience, conservative record and Washington relationships make him the more effective advocate.

## Why it matters nationally

Nebraska hasn't elected a non-Republican senator since Democrat Ben Nelson won reelection in 2006, and the Republican Nelson beat that year was Pete Ricketts himself. Twenty years later Ricketts faces another strong challenger from outside his party.

An Osborn win would be one of the most unusual outcomes of 2026 and a test of whether economically populist independents can win in deep-red states where the Democratic label is toxic, with implications for places like Kansas, Iowa and the Dakotas.

## What to watch

Nebraska polls close at 8 p.m. Central (7 p.m. Mountain in the panhandle), 9 p.m. Eastern. Early and absentee ballots may dominate many counties' first reports, so early leads may mislead. Per the source, Nebraska requires an automatic recount when a statewide margin is 1% or less. Watch Osborn's margins in Omaha and Lincoln, his performance in suburbs around Omaha, and whether he can improve substantially on typical Democratic results in rural Nebraska.

The governor's race, between Republican Gov. Jim Pillen and the Democratic nominee, is a useful comparison: if Osborn clearly outruns that candidate, it would suggest his independent, working-class identity reached voters beyond the usual Democratic coalition.
