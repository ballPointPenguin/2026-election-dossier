Georgia is one of the most important Democratic-held Senate seats on the ballot. First-term Democrat Jon Ossoff is seeking reelection in a state Donald Trump narrowly won in 2024, against two-term Republican Rep. Mike Collins from northeastern Georgia. Unlike Alaska or Florida, where Democrats are trying to flip Republican seats, this is mostly a defensive race: losing it would make an already hard path to a Senate majority much harder.

The question has been whether Ossoff can repeat his 2021 coalition: big Democratic margins in metro Atlanta, strong Black turnout, and enough suburban moderates to offset rural Republican strength.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jon Ossoff | Democrat | Incumbent; elected in the January 2021 runoff at 33; former investigative journalist and documentary producer; stresses accountability, health care, constituent services and opposition to Trump administration policies |
| Mike Collins | Republican | Congressman since 2023 (GA-10, east of Atlanta); former trucking-company executive; staunch Trump ally and immigration hardliner; won a contentious primary runoff over former football coach Derek Dooley |

Ossoff has built an image as a pragmatic, energetic senator focused on oversight and anti-corruption. Collins runs a confrontational, populist campaign casting Ossoff as an out-of-touch liberal tied to inflation and immigration. Collins sponsored the Laken Riley Act, named for the Georgia nursing student killed by a Venezuelan migrant in 2024, which Trump signed in 2025. Ossoff attacks Collins for his Trump alignment and for controversies over former staffers linked to white nationalist material; Collins has condemned white supremacy and says those staffers were fired.

## How the race changed

Early in the cycle Ossoff was widely seen as the most vulnerable Democratic incumbent. Four things changed that:

- **A bruising Republican primary.** Collins beat Dooley, whom Gov. Brian Kemp backed, and Rep. Buddy Carter, winning the runoff with Trump's support, but the fight exposed the split between Trump-aligned and Kemp-aligned Republicans.
- **A huge money edge.** Through October 14, Ossoff had about $136.6M in candidate receipts to Collins's $10.7M, nearly 13 to 1, not counting outside spending.
- **Republicans left.** The NRSC prioritized other battlegrounds, and the Senate Leadership Fund canceled millions in Georgia ad reservations, a sign national Republicans see better chances elsewhere.
- **Strong approval.** The October AJC poll had Ossoff at 55% approval statewide and 61% among independents; 13% of self-described conservatives backed him, against 4% of liberals for Collins.

## Georgia's geography

| Region | Significance |
| --- | --- |
| Atlanta: Fulton and DeKalb | Enormous Democratic margins; turnout is essential |
| Cobb and Gwinnett suburbs | Former Republican strongholds, increasingly Democratic |
| Henry, Clayton and Douglas counties | Important Black and suburban Democratic constituencies |
| Savannah and Augusta | Democratic-leaning cities in Republican territory |
| North Georgia | Strongly Republican outside metro areas |
| Rural South Georgia | Mostly Republican, with substantial Black Democratic communities |

The dynamic is whether metro Atlanta's large and growing electorate can outweigh Republican margins everywhere else. Unlike Florida, Georgia has seen sustained Democratic improvement in its biggest suburban counties, which keeps it a genuine battleground despite rural conservatism.

## Ossoff's first win

Ossoff won the January 5, 2021 runoff over Republican David Perdue by about 1.2 points, the same night Raphael Warnock beat Kelly Loeffler, which gave Democrats the 50–50 Senate with Vice President Kamala Harris breaking ties. Warnock won reelection in 2022 over Herschel Walker. A second Ossoff win would make four straight Georgia Senate victories for Democrats since 2021, even as Republicans win other statewide races.

## The runoff rule

A candidate needs more than 50% to win outright. If nobody gets there, the top two meet in a runoff on December 1, 2026. Ossoff and Collins are the real contenders, with some write-ins on record and no major third-party candidate likely to force one. With Ossoff polling above 50%, an election-night win looks far likelier than a December runoff.

## Why it matters nationally

- **A must-hold.** Georgia is a necessary piece of any plausible Democratic Senate majority, but the party also needs substantial gains in Republican-held states.
- **Republican priorities shifted.** They once hoped to take Ossoff's seat; now they appear to be defending their own vulnerable seats and chasing better targets.
- **Ossoff's profile.** Winning twice in a state still competitive for president would strengthen his reputation as a coalition-builder. His name comes up for 2028, though he has denied presidential ambitions.

## What to watch

Georgia polls close at 7 p.m. Eastern, and more than 2.8 million people have already voted, a state midterm record. The question isn't just whether Ossoff wins but by how much: a narrow win would show Georgia is still tight even in a favorable national environment, while a margin of eight to ten points would be a large Democratic overperformance in a Trump-won state.

Compare it with the governor's race, where Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms faces Republican Rick Jackson and recent polling shows a much closer contest. If Ossoff runs well ahead of Bottoms, that would suggest his personal popularity, incumbency and organization mattered beyond the national swing.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
