Mississippi's underlying alignment strongly favors Republicans. Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith is seeking a second full six-year term, and Democrats face a hard task in a state Donald Trump carried by about 22 points in 2024. But Mississippi is more complicated than its presidential results suggest: it has one of the country's largest Black electorates, regional pockets of Democratic strength, and deep socioeconomic problems that give an opening to campaigns about health care, poverty and economic development.

The race matters less for Senate control than for what it shows about Democrats' difficulties in the Deep South even in a favorable national year.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cindy Hyde-Smith | Republican | Incumbent since 2018; former state agriculture commissioner and former Democratic state legislator; strong Trump ally stressing agriculture, conservative judges, immigration enforcement and spending priorities |
| Scott Colom | Democrat | District attorney for Mississippi's 16th Circuit, covering four northeastern counties; first Black DA elected in that district; campaigns on affordability, health care, economic opportunity and accountability |
| Ty Pinkins | Independent | Army veteran and attorney; the 2024 Democratic Senate nominee, now running independently on poverty, voting rights, rural development and dissatisfaction with both parties |

All three are on the official general-election ballot.

## A personal rivalry

President Biden nominated Colom for a federal district judgeship in 2023, but Hyde-Smith opposed the nomination, effectively blocking it through the Senate's home-state consultation tradition. Colom then entered the race against the senator who had opposed him. It gives the campaign a personal edge and shows the power senators hold over judicial appointments.

## Geography

Mississippi is one of the most racially polarized electorates in the country, and about 38% of residents are Black, the largest share of any state. Democrats win overwhelming Black support but lose white voters by huge margins, so Colom must mobilize Black voters while winning a larger minority of white voters than recent Democrats have. A few points of white crossover can have an outsized effect.

| Region | Significance |
| --- | --- |
| Jackson / Hinds County | Major Democratic stronghold; Black turnout critical |
| Mississippi Delta | Heavily Black and Democratic, but population loss and turnout challenges |
| Columbus / Golden Triangle | Colom's home territory and base |
| Oxford / Lafayette County | University-centered electorate with Democratic potential |
| DeSoto County / Memphis suburbs | Strongly Republican, with some suburban persuasion room |
| Gulf Coast / Harrison and Jackson counties | Large Republican vote reservoirs |
| Rankin and Madison counties | Republican suburban strongholds around Jackson |

## Not entirely out of reach

Hyde-Smith herself hasn't won by margins like Trump's:

| --- | --- |
| 2018 Senate special runoff | R+7.3 |
| 2020 Senate | R+10 |
| 2024 president | about R+22 |
| 2024 Senate (Roger Wicker) | R+25.6 |

She beat former Democratic Rep. Mike Espy in 2018 and 2020 by much smaller margins than many other statewide Republicans, which suggests some candidate-specific vulnerability, though turnout and opponents also explain it. Mississippi hasn't elected a Democratic senator since John Stennis's final win in 1982, and Stennis belonged to the old segregationist Southern Democratic tradition, so a Colom victory would be a realignment, not a restoration.

## The issues

- **Affordability and health care** anchor Colom's campaign: he says Republican policies have deepened hardship for lower-income households and rural communities that depend on Medicaid and other federal programs.
- **Hyde-Smith** stresses tax cuts, deregulation, immigration enforcement and her role representing Mississippi agriculture.
- **Farms, tariffs and the Iran war.** Farmers face production costs, trade uncertainty and the Iran conflict's economic effects, and Colom ties these to national Republican policy. Hyde-Smith defends Trump's military policy while acknowledging the conflict has pushed prices up.
- **Abortion.** Hyde-Smith has consistently opposed abortion rights and opposed a 2024 federal IVF-protection bill, saying its language went beyond protecting fertility treatment. Colom is more supportive of reproductive rights.

## A high-profile campaign

National figures have come to Mississippi: Trump to support Hyde-Smith, and Bill Clinton and Kamala Harris for Colom, with Barack Obama also involved in the Democratic effort. That is extraordinary attention for a state normally written off. It doesn't mean Democratic strategists think Colom will probably win, but it suggests they see value in stretching Republican resources, mobilizing voters and exploiting a favorable year.

## Why it matters nationally

- **A big upset if it happens.** A Colom win would rival or exceed Democratic upsets in Kansas and Iowa, and would make him Mississippi's first Black U.S. senator since Reconstruction.
- **Even a narrow loss says something.** A Republican win of only three to six points in a state Trump carried by more than twenty would suggest a large Democratic swing reaching the Deep South.
- **Longer term.** Mississippi elects a governor in 2027, and a strong Colom showing could make him a credible future statewide contender even if he loses.

The Change Research poll surveyed 842 likely voters (±3.5 points) for Government That Works PAC, with 9% unsure and 2% saying they wouldn't vote. A polling-based forecast has Hyde-Smith winning by about 7. Hyde-Smith remains the favorite, but the polls point to a closer race than expected, and Colom has a credible outside chance if Democratic turnout is exceptionally strong and some Republicans stay home.

## What to watch

Mississippi polls close at 7 p.m. Central, 8 p.m. Eastern. Most voting happens on Election Day, since the state has restrictive absentee rules and no general early in-person voting. Watch Black turnout in Hinds County and the Delta, Colom's margins in the Golden Triangle, and whether Republican turnout falls short on the Gulf Coast and in the Jackson suburbs.

Pinkins is a real variable. At roughly 6–7% he could materially affect the margin, though it isn't safe to assume his voters would otherwise back Democrats.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
