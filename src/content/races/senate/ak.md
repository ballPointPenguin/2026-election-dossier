Alaska has become one of the most consequential Senate battlegrounds of 2026. Two-term Republican Dan Sullivan faces former Democratic congresswoman Mary Peltola, who has a real ability to attract independents and Republican-leaning Alaskans. She enters Election Day with a narrow polling edge, but the race is still very competitive. An upset here could be essential to Democrats' hopes of retaking the Senate.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mary Peltola | Democrat | Challenger; U.S. representative 2022–2025; first Alaska Native elected to Congress; fisheries advocate with a moderate, cross-partisan brand |
| Dan Sullivan | Republican | Incumbent seeking a third term; senator since 2015; former Alaska attorney general and Marine Corps officer; stresses defense, energy and seniority |
| Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. | Independent | Retired teacher who shares the incumbent's name (see below) |
| Gerald Heikes | Republican | Advanced to the general election after another candidate withdrew |

## The state

Alaska leans Republican for president (Trump +13 in 2024) but has a more independent, regional and personality-driven politics than most red states. It elected Democratic Sen. Mark Begich in 2008, Republican Lisa Murkowski has survived challenges from her party's right, and Peltola won the at-large House seat in 2022 before losing it in 2024 to Nick Begich III (see [AK-AL](../../house/ak-al/)).

- **Alaska Native voters:** Peltola's Yup'ik heritage and rural ties matter most in western and southwestern Alaska.
- **Anchorage and suburbs:** the biggest pool of votes and the key battleground for moderates.
- **Fairbanks and the Mat-Su Valley:** Republican strongholds, though they differ in makeup.
- **Resource politics:** fishing, oil, gas, federal land and subsistence rights cut across national party lines.
- **Independents:** a large unaffiliated electorate makes candidate identity unusually important.

Peltola's "Fish, Family, Freedom" brand fits the state. She isn't running as a conventional national progressive: she talks about local industries, bipartisanship and Alaska's autonomy. Sullivan has incumbency, ties to the energy and defense sectors, and a state that usually votes Republican.

## Ranked-choice voting

Alaska uses a top-four primary and ranked-choice voting in November. The August primary:

| Candidate | Primary vote |
| --- | --- |
| Mary Peltola (D) | 49.5% |
| Dan Sullivan (R) | 41.4% |
| Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. (I) | 2.5% |
| David Leslie (D) | 1.1% |

Leslie later withdrew, which let fifth-place finisher Gerald Heikes advance. Peltola's near-50% showing was strong, though primary electorates don't match November's. If nobody tops 50% of first preferences, transfers from minor candidates and the number of exhausted ballots could decide a close race. Ranked-choice tabulation in Alaska doesn't begin until November 18.

## The two-Dan Sullivan controversy

One of the stranger stories of the cycle: New York Times reporting indicated that people associated with Peltola's campaign helped recruit a second candidate named Dan Sullivan, apparently hoping to confuse voters and pull support from the incumbent. Peltola has denied knowing of any such scheme.

Ranked-choice voting limits the trick: a voter who mistakenly ranks the independent first can still rank the incumbent second, and election experts consider a decisive spoiler effect unlikely. The reporting is still the sort of thing that feeds a legitimacy fight in a close race.

The other legal sideshow: on October 23 the Trump administration's Justice Department sued to prevent certification of the Senate result under certain circumstances if the Republican incumbent lost. A federal judge dismissed the suit on November 1, and the department said it would appeal. Sullivan has said publicly that he will accept the results.

Together the two stories add a legitimacy question to a race that was already close, whatever their real effect on the vote.

The number shows how much national Democratic attention the race draws, and it matters in a state where reaching dispersed communities is expensive.

## Why it matters nationally

- **A pivotal pickup.** Republicans began the cycle with 53 seats, so Democrats need a net gain of four for 51, assuming Democratic-aligned independents caucus with them. Alaska wasn't the obvious target at first, but Peltola put it alongside Maine, Ohio, Iowa, Kansas and Texas.
- **A test of local brand.** Can a Democrat win a strongly Republican presidential state on local identity, moderate positioning and discontent with the national Republican administration?
- **Same ballot as [AK-AL](../../house/ak-al/).** Begich's House race is the other side of Peltola's old seat.

## What to watch

Peltola's margins in Anchorage, turnout in rural Alaska Native communities, and whether Sullivan consolidates Republicans in the Mat-Su Valley, Fairbanks and other conservative areas. Rural ballots count slowly and ranked-choice tabulation comes later, so a close race could stay unresolved well past election night.
