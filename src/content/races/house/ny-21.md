New York's 21st Congressional District has become one of the more interesting Republican-held seats in the 2026 midterms. It is a heavily Republican, predominantly rural district in northern New York, represented by Elise Stefanik since 2015.

But Stefanik is retiring, leaving an open seat. The contest features two candidates who have never served in Congress: a Trump-endorsed businessman and a Democratic dairy farmer.

The noteworthy development is that recent polling shows a competitive race in a district Donald Trump carried by approximately 21 percentage points in 2024. National election analysts have consequently shifted their assessments of the contest toward greater competitiveness.

This October 9 Siena Research Institute release is especially interesting because it also found:

- Voters preferred the Republican candidate for governor by 55% to 40% in the same district.
- Voters preferred Republican control of Congress, 50% to 45%.
- Gendebien had a 46% favorable versus 29% unfavorable rating.
- Constantino had a 39% favorable versus 52% unfavorable rating.

That suggests a distinction between the district's underlying partisan orientation and voters' evaluations of the individual congressional candidates.

The polls have not all agreed. An August J.L. Partners survey measured Constantino at 53% and Gendebien at 40%, whereas an August SurveyUSA poll measured Gendebien at 43% and Constantino at 39%. Different samples, timing and methodologies limit direct comparisons.

## 2. The district: Adirondacks, dairy country, and the Canadian border

NY-21 is one of the geographically largest congressional districts east of the Mississippi. It covers much of New York's North Country and the Adirondacks, extending from the Canadian border toward the Mohawk Valley.

It encompasses Plattsburgh, Lake Placid, Glens Falls, Amsterdam and numerous small towns and rural communities.

The district's economy is unusually varied for such a rural region: dairy agriculture, forestry, tourism, manufacturing, military employment, and trade with Canada all matter.

This is not simply an affluent suburban swing district. Its political concerns have a distinctly North Country character:

- **Agriculture:** Farm consolidation, dairy prices, operating costs, and access to markets.
- **Healthcare:** Maintaining hospitals and medical services across a sparsely populated region.
- **Canada:** Border security, tariffs, tourism, and cross-border commercial relationships.
- **Demography:** Population loss, aging communities, and difficulty retaining younger workers.
- **Infrastructure:** Rural broadband, transportation, and reliable energy.

A particularly interesting historical fact: Democrats represented this territory relatively recently. Bill Owens won a 2009 special election and served until 2015, when Stefanik took office. The district has since become considerably more Republican in its federal voting patterns.

## 3. The candidates and their campaigns

### Anthony Constantino — Republican

Constantino, 43, founded Sticker Mule, an international custom-printing company headquartered in Amsterdam, New York. He defeated state Assemblymember Robert Smullen in the Republican primary, despite Smullen's substantial support from local party organizations.

Constantino received Donald Trump's endorsement and has closely associated his campaign with the president. His priorities include manufacturing jobs, border security, economic development, and a government technology modernization proposal called Operation Overhaul.

Constantino has employed unconventional advertising, including AI-generated videos and political music. An August AI video portraying Gendebien making statements he never made generated controversy about synthetic campaign media. Constantino defended it as a response to earlier AI material from Gendebien's campaign.

### Blake Gendebien — Democrat

Gendebien is a dairy farmer from Lisbon in St. Lawrence County. He has served on a local school board and in agricultural cooperative leadership.

He defeated Lake Placid entrepreneur Stuart Amoriell in the Democratic primary. His campaign emphasizes reducing household costs, preserving rural healthcare, supporting farmers, and improving public infrastructure.

Gendebien identifies as a conservative Democrat and a free-market capitalist, emphasizing economic issues and local concerns over national ideological disputes.

Gendebien began campaigning in late 2024 when Stefanik was initially nominated to become U.S. ambassador to the United Nations. That nomination was withdrawn, but he continued organizing, giving his campaign a lengthy head start before the open-seat contest was confirmed.

### Christopher Schmidt — Independent write-in

Schmidt, a Washington County community organizer, is also campaigning, emphasizing employment, infrastructure, utilities, and rural healthcare. He did not qualify for a printed ballot line, making his candidacy a write-in effort. His 5% in the Siena survey is noteworthy, but that survey support cannot be assumed to translate directly into actual write-in votes.

## 4. Campaign financing

September 30 figures reported by City & State New York; approximately $4.1 million and $470,000 remained in the respective campaign accounts.

Constantino has committed substantial personal wealth to the campaign. The financial disparity is relevant to advertising capacity, but it is not a direct measure of grassroots support or voter intentions.

The Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee also added NY-21 to its competitive-race program in early October, bringing additional national attention and resources.

## 5. Why this race matters nationally

Three larger political questions make NY-21 significant beyond its single House seat.

First, it is a test of candidate-specific voting in a strongly partisan district. The October Siena poll found Gendebien attracting 21% of Republican respondents, while Constantino attracted 65% of Republicans. Independents favored Gendebien 51% to 30%. These findings raise an important question about how much individual candidate evaluations can alter established partisan voting patterns.

Second, it highlights tensions within the Republican coalition. The primary pitted Smullen, an experienced state legislator backed by numerous county Republican committees, against Constantino, who received Trump's endorsement. Constantino's nomination reflected a different constituency within the party than the one supporting Smullen.

Third, it is part of the national contest for control of the House. Every competitive Republican-held seat matters to the arithmetic of a possible change in congressional control. NY-21 is especially notable because it was not initially among the districts attracting the most attention.

There is also a distinctly local dimension: relations with Canada. In the Siena survey, respondents preferred Gendebien over Constantino, 52% to 35%, on handling those relations. That issue carries particular weight in a district economically connected to Quebec and Ontario.

## 6. What to watch between now and November 3

The remaining questions concern whether late-deciding voters follow their usual partisan preferences, whether Constantino's considerable financial resources change the campaign's closing dynamics, and whether Schmidt's write-in candidacy attracts meaningful votes.

The difference between a four-point polling lead and an actual election result is considerable, particularly when the sample is fewer than 500 likely voters and the district has such a pronounced Republican voting history.

For continued coverage, the most useful sources are [North Country Public Radio](https://www.northcountrypublicradio.org/), [WAMC Northeast Public Radio](https://www.wamc.org/), the [October Siena poll and crosstabs](https://sri.siena.edu/2026/10/09/times-union-wptz-nbc5-wamc-siena-poll-of-likely-ny-21-voters/), and [Cook Political Report's NY-21 race page](https://www.cookpolitical.com/house/race/483676).

The central political story is the divergence between party preference and candidate preference. NY-21's historical voting patterns, registration figures, and presidential results all demonstrate a substantial Republican constituency. Yet the latest independent survey records significantly different attitudes toward the two congressional nominees. Whether that divergence persists through Election Day is the defining empirical question of this race.
