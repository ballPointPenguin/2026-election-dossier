Alaska's single at-large district is one of the most unusual contests of the 2026 midterms. Republican incumbent Nick Begich III faces a surprisingly competitive challenge from Bill Hill, an independent commercial fisherman and former school superintendent who has become the principal alternative for Democratic and independent voters.

The question is whether Hill can assemble Democrats, independents, Alaska Native voters and moderate Republicans into a coalition big enough to beat Begich's incumbency and Alaska's Republican presidential lean. Alaska's ranked-choice voting adds a wrinkle: the winner may not be determined by first-choice totals alone. Polling suggests Begich is still favored, but Cook and Sabato have moved the race from Likely to Lean Republican.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nick Begich III | Republican | Incumbent since 2025; age 49; Anchorage businessman and technology entrepreneur |
| Bill Hill | Independent | Bristol Bay commercial and subsistence fisherman; former Bristol Bay Borough school superintendent; DCCC Red to Blue |
| Eric Hafner | Democrat | On the ballot (see below) |
| Jim McDermott | Libertarian | Retired Air Force master sergeant and business instructor, Fairbanks |

## The district

Alaska has had one at-large representative since statehood in 1959, covering more than 570,000 square miles.

- **Anchorage:** the largest population center; suburban, military, professional and working-class.
- **Mat-Su Borough:** fast-growing communities around Wasilla and Palmer, generally a Republican stronghold.
- **Fairbanks and the Interior:** university, military, government, energy and resource communities.
- **Juneau and Southeast:** state government, fishing, tourism, maritime commerce, comparatively strong Democratic areas.
- **Bristol Bay and western Alaska:** commercial fishing, Alaska Native communities, rural education, subsistence economies.
- **North Slope and Arctic:** Alaska Native communities, oil production, subsistence traditions, major infrastructure challenges.

Alaska is generally Republican for president, but its political culture has long made room for independents and candidates who diverge from national parties.

| Election | Winner | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1973–2022 | Don Young (R) | Nearly five decades of Republican representation |
| 2022 special election | Mary Peltola (D) | First Alaska Native in Congress |
| 2022 general | Peltola (D) | Won under ranked-choice voting |
| 2024 general | Begich (R) | Beat Peltola by about 3.4 points |

Peltola's 2022 wins showed Alaska's presidential preference doesn't automatically decide its House races; her 2024 loss showed that crossover coalition's limits. In 2026 she's running against Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan, so Alaska's House and Senate races may affect each other through turnout and organization.

## Candidates

### Nick Begich III (R)

He won in 2024 after two unsuccessful tries in 2022. His surname has an unusual history: his grandfather, Nick Begich Sr., a Democratic congressman, disappeared in a 1972 plane crash with House Majority Leader Hale Boggs, and his uncle Mark Begich later served as a Democratic U.S. senator, though Nick III is a conservative Republican. He stresses expanding Alaska oil, gas and mineral production, faster federal permitting, less regulation, military installations and commercial fisheries. He argues federal landholdings constrain development, housing and extraction, and supports Republican tax policy. His campaign stresses working within the Republican majority to deliver federal support for infrastructure and resource industries.

### Bill Hill (I)

A lifelong Alaskan, Hill stresses independence from national party leadership. His campaign centers on the cost of living, rural healthcare, public education, fisheries protection, infrastructure and government responsiveness to remote communities. Salmon runs, bycatch, fisheries management and habitat are major economic and cultural issues, and he has opposed the proposed Pebble Mine in the Bristol Bay watershed and urged stronger science and habitat protection for salmon.

Many Democrats see an independent as a stronger vehicle for a broad anti-incumbent coalition. Democrat Matt Schultz, who first sought the seat, suspended his campaign and endorsed Hill, and the DCCC added Hill to its Red to Blue program.

### The other candidates

Both matter because of ranked-choice voting. Eric Hafner (D) is a highly unusual nominee: he is serving a federal prison sentence in New York yet reached the general ballot after other candidates withdrew, and has no meaningful conventional campaign. Jim McDermott (L) also advanced after withdrawals. Their first-choice voters' later rankings could affect the result if Begich and Hill are close.

## The primary and ranked-choice voting

Alaska's August 18 primary lets voters choose among candidates regardless of party; the top four were initially eligible to advance, but withdrawals changed the November lineup.

| Candidate | August primary share |
| --- | --- |
| Nick Begich III (R) | 44.6% |
| Bill Hill (I) | 32.5% |
| Matthew Schultz (D, withdrawn) | 8.1% |
| Eric Hafner (D) | 3.8% |
| John Brendan Williams (D, withdrawn) | 2.7% |

Begich finished about 12 points ahead of Hill, but several of the others weren't continuing, so the primary margin is an imperfect predictor.

**How ranked-choice voting works.** Voters may rank candidates. If no candidate has a majority of active first-choice votes, the last-place candidate is eliminated and their ballots transfer to the next-ranked continuing candidate, repeating until someone has a majority of active votes. Hill doesn't have to lead first choices to win; he needs enough transfers from eliminated candidates' voters. That isn't automatic: some Hafner voters may rank Begich next or leave no usable next choice, and some McDermott voters may prefer Hill.

An illustration, not a prediction: with first choices of Begich 47%, Hill 43%, Hafner 7% and McDermott 3%, if all ballots stay active and transfers produce a 51–49 final result, that's a very close finish despite a four-point first-choice gap. Real transfers may differ, and ballots with no further usable ranking can become exhausted.

Begich has a large edge in direct fundraising, but Hill gets far more outside help: about **$21.75 million** in independent expenditures through October 31 (about $18.10M in the general election, $3.65M in primaries), roughly $11.6M supporting Hill or opposing Begich and $5.9M the other way (rounded, excluding some minor-candidate spending). So Begich has the better-funded organization while Hill benefits from about twice the outside spending, and DCCC support shows Hill is effectively the principal opposition candidate without a Democratic label.

## The issues

**Fisheries, salmon and bycatch.** A rare congressional race where fisheries are central. Both candidates want to reduce bycatch (unintended capture of salmon, halibut, crab and other species) and improve management. Begich points to legislation he introduced on monitoring, gear standards and management. Hill stresses direct fishing experience, stronger bycatch limits, Alaska Native subsistence rights and more tribal representation on the North Pacific Fishery Management Council. At their September Kodiak debate they agreed on a lot, though Hill more explicitly stressed climate change and opposition to industrial practices that harm small communities.

**Oil, gas, minerals and federal land.** Begich strongly backs expanding resource development, cutting permitting barriers and more access to federal land. Hill stresses development alongside environmental protection, especially where extraction threatens fisheries and subsistence. The proposed Pebble Mine near Bristol Bay illustrates the tension with valuable salmon habitat.

**Healthcare and cost of living.** Alaska faces high transportation, energy, food and housing costs, with remote communities dependent on air transport and fuel deliveries, and healthcare often requiring travel. Begich favors local food production, housing opportunities and resource-based growth; Hill stresses healthcare access, education, affordability and federal policy that accounts for remote communities' costs.

**The Iran war and congressional authority.** Begich supports Trump's use of force against Iran, citing nuclear and security threats. Hill opposes the war, argues Congress has surrendered too much authority over military action and tariffs, and ties the conflict to higher fuel and transportation costs in Alaska.

## Why it matters nationally

- **An independent as principal challenger.** Can voters who usually back Democrats unite behind a candidate with no major-party label?
- **Ranked-choice voting under pressure.** Alaskans are also considering a ballot measure to repeal it, so this race may be a visible example of how it affects outcomes.
- **The Peltola factor.** She's running for the Senate, which could affect turnout among Alaska Native voters, independents and moderate Republicans, though voters may split tickets.
- **Rural and Indigenous coalitions.** Bristol Bay, the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta and other rural regions have priorities that don't map neatly onto national party lines.
- **House balance.** A Hill win would be an opposition pickup from a Republican incumbent, but as an independent his caucus affiliation would matter for the House's partisan balance.

## What to watch on election night

The Anchorage vote, turnout in Alaska Native and rural communities, Hill's strength in Bristol Bay and coastal fishing towns, and Republican margins in Mat-Su, plus Hafner's and McDermott's first-choice shares and whether their voters' later preferences help Hill close the gap. Alaska's remote geography and absentee processing mean a close race may stay unresolved beyond election night, and ranked-choice tabulation can add more time.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026.
