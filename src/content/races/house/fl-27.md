Florida's 27th District, in Miami-Dade County, is a Republican-held seat that has tightened late in the cycle. Incumbent María Elvira Salazar, first elected in 2020, faces Eliott Rodriguez, a longtime Miami news anchor. Both are Cuban American former TV journalists. Salazar won in 2024 with 60.4%, about 21 points, in a district Trump carried by roughly 15, and all three forecasters now rate the race Lean Republican.

Immigration is the main event. Both candidates are well known to Miami's Hispanic electorate, but they respond differently to the Trump administration's enforcement policies, especially the end of temporary protections for Venezuelan, Haitian and Nicaraguan migrants.

## The district

FL-27 is almost entirely urban and suburban: parts of Miami, Little Havana, Coral Gables, South Miami, Pinecrest, Kendall, Palmetto Bay and Cutler Bay.

Miami's Cuban American electorate has been shaped by anti-Castro politics, exile communities, opposition to authoritarian governments in Latin America and strong Republican institutions. But the Hispanic population is not a bloc. Venezuelan, Nicaraguan, Colombian and other communities have different migration histories, economic circumstances and relationships to U.S. immigration policy. The district also mixes affluent neighborhoods with places squeezed by housing costs, so the issues range from Latin American foreign policy and immigration status to affordability and development.

It used to be Democratic territory: Donna Shalala held it from 2019 to 2021 before Salazar beat her, and it then moved sharply right.

## Candidates

### María Elvira Salazar (R)

Salazar, 64, reported extensively on Latin America in Spanish-language television before entering politics, and is seeking a fourth term. She won the August primary over attorney Michael Arias with about 81%. Her priorities are economic development, small business, public safety, immigration reform and opposition to authoritarian governments in Latin America.

Her immigration stance is a mix: she backs border enforcement but champions the bipartisan Dignity Act, which would create a path to legal status for certain long-term undocumented residents alongside enforcement measures. In September she publicly criticized parts of the administration's enforcement, citing arrests of people without criminal records and a sense of betrayal among some Hispanic Trump voters. Trump then reiterated his support for her.

### Eliott Rodriguez (D)

Rodriguez, 69, retired from CBS4/WFOR in December 2025 after decades in Miami TV news. He beat former federal prosecutor Robin Peguero in the primary, 54% to 46%. He runs on affordable housing, household costs, Medicare and Social Security, constitutional protections, and a more humane immigration system alongside border security. He argues enforcement has eroded trust in government among mixed-status families, and that Miami's housing costs are pushing younger generations out.

A fun detail: both candidates interviewed Fidel Castro during their journalism careers, and both now compete in a district where Cuban exile history still carries political weight.

## Why it matters nationally

**Immigration enforcement and the Hispanic vote.** Trump gained with Hispanic voters in 2024, including in Miami-Dade. In 2026, enforcement, particularly the end of Temporary Protected Status for several nationalities, has worried some of the same communities. The New Yorker has described families affected by detentions and lost protections, and some residents who previously voted Republican becoming politically engaged. Salazar's move is to criticize some enforcement practices while staying a Republican who supports broader immigration controls; Rodriguez argues that isn't enough without a change in representation. The underlying question is how voters separate border security, deportation priorities, humanitarian protections and the treatment of established immigrant communities.

**The South Florida Republican coalition.** Republican gains in South Florida were among the major regional shifts of the past decade, and FL-27 is a chance to see whether that coalition holds in a different environment: longtime Cuban American Republicans, newer immigrant communities, independents, suburban professionals and younger residents facing high housing costs. Shifts in turnout among any of these could matter even without big changes in party identification.

**National attention.** On October 16, House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries campaigned with Rodriguez in Pinecrest, a sign that Democratic leadership sees a district Salazar won comfortably two years ago as in play. With a closely divided House, seats like this feed into the fight for control.

## What to watch

Whether polling captures Miami's likely electorate, how voters react to Salazar's public disagreements with the administration, and whether Rodriguez can turn name recognition into votes.
