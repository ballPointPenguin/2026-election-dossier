Iowa's 1st District covers southeastern Iowa, including Iowa City, Davenport, Burlington and much of the Quad Cities, and has produced remarkably close elections. This is the third straight matchup between Republican incumbent Mariannette Miller-Meeks and Democrat Christina Bohannan, and this time independent Michael Bridgford is also on the ballot.

The defining fact: Miller-Meeks won in 2024 by just 799 votes out of more than 414,000. This year adds a well-funded independent, heavy national spending, and a political environment that has led analysts to revise their ratings.

## The district

IA-1 spans 20 counties, joining several Democratic-voting population centers with small towns and Republican rural areas.

| Region | Character |
| --- | --- |
| Iowa City / Johnson County | University of Iowa, healthcare, research, students |
| Davenport / Scott County | Manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, Mississippi River commerce |
| Burlington / Des Moines County | Manufacturing, transportation, industrial history |
| Muscatine County | Agriculture, food processing, manufacturing |
| Rural southeast Iowa | Corn, soybeans, livestock, small industry |

Miller-Meeks won 17 of the 20 counties in 2024 and still barely won, because big margins in a few population centers can offset wins across many small counties. Formerly competitive or Democratic industrial areas have become more Republican nationally, while Iowa City remains a major Democratic anchor.

## Candidates

### Mariannette Miller-Meeks (R)

A physician, Army veteran and former state senator, she works on healthcare, veterans, agriculture and energy. She backs much of the Republican tax and economic agenda but has broken with the administration at times: she was one of seven House Republicans who voted to limit Trump's military actions in Iran, citing Congress's constitutional role and worries about a long war and higher fuel prices.

### Christina Bohannan (D)

Bohannan, a law professor and former state representative, challenged Miller-Meeks in 2022 and 2024 and won the 2026 primary with about 81%. She emphasizes household affordability, tariffs, healthcare, reproductive rights and accountability, with much more focus than in 2024 on prices and the economic effects of trade policy.

### Michael Bridgford (I)

Bridgford campaigns on dissatisfaction with both parties and the need for bipartisan problem-solving, arguing that alternating control hasn't fixed major problems. Unlike most independents, he has real money and measurable polling support.

## Why the last election matters

| 2024 House, IA-1 | Votes |
| --- | --- |
| Miller-Meeks (R) | 206,955 |
| Bohannan (D) | 206,156 |

A 799-vote margin, about 0.19 points. Miller-Meeks beat Bohannan by about 6.8 points in 2022, and she won her first election in 2020 by six votes in the former 2nd District after a recount, which made her a familiar name in discussions of election administration. Iowa's lines changed between 2020 and 2022, so the six-vote race isn't a direct baseline for this district.

A VoteLog compilation of October 23 shows IA-1 as the most expensive of Iowa's four House races.

## The issues

**Tariffs, farming and affordability.** Bohannan argues tariffs raise groceries, fuel and other costs and create business uncertainty. Miller-Meeks supports parts of the administration's economic agenda while acknowledging that higher fuel prices pinch Iowa farmers. Producers, processors and manufacturers can be hit by both import costs and retaliatory trade measures.

**The Iran war.** Miller-Meeks voted with six other Republicans to limit Trump's military actions there, arguing Iran shouldn't get nuclear weapons but Congress must exercise its role. Bohannan criticizes the approach and its economic costs. It's a less party-line comparison than usual.

**Cancer, water and healthcare.** Iowa's elevated cancer rates and possible environmental exposures are unusually prominent. Iowa Public Radio interviewed both candidates in September on cancer prevention, research and treatment access. Agricultural runoff puts nitrates and phosphorus in waterways, and research continues, though no single explanation for Iowa's cancer rates is established.

## Why it matters nationally

- **Extreme closeness.** 799 votes in 2024, six in 2020 under the old map.
- **A third matchup.** Voters know both major-party candidates well.
- **An independent with weight.** Bridgford complicates comparisons with the two-way 2022 and 2024 results.
- **Money.** More than $17M in outside spending.
- **Iowa's bigger picture.** An open Senate seat plus [IA-2](../ia-02/) and [IA-3](../ia-03/); IA-1's repeat rivals make it a particularly clean comparison.

Bridgford's share could matter in a district decided by 799 votes, but his voters can't be assumed to lean either way.

## What to watch

Another independent three-candidate poll, final spending reports, and turnout in Johnson, Scott and Jefferson counties relative to the rural counties.
