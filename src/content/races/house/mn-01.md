Minnesota's 1st District in southern Minnesota is one of the late-developing contests of the 2026 midterms. Trump carried it by about 12 points in 2024 and Republican Brad Finstad won reelection by roughly 17, yet several public polls now show it nearly even. All four major forecasters rate it Lean Republican, up from Likely or Solid Republican earlier in the year.

## The district

MN-1 spans southern Minnesota from the South Dakota border to the Mississippi River and Wisconsin line. Its main population centers are Rochester, Mankato, Winona, Austin, Albert Lea and Worthington.

- **Rochester and Olmsted County:** Mayo Clinic, healthcare, research, education, professionals.
- **Mankato and North Mankato:** higher education, regional healthcare, manufacturing.
- **Austin and Albert Lea:** food processing, manufacturing, agriculture, organized labor.
- **Worthington and the southwest:** agriculture, meatpacking, immigrant communities.
- **Winona and the river counties:** manufacturing, colleges, river commerce.
- **Rural counties:** corn, soybeans, livestock, equipment, ag services.

Rochester and the college towns are a very different electorate from the heavily agricultural counties farther west.

Cook rates the district R+6 on its Partisan Voting Index, a presidential-vote measure relative to the nation, not a forecast. MN-1 isn't historically lopsided: Democrat Tim Walz held the seat from 2007 to 2019, including years when southern Minnesota voted Republican for president, which shows the district can elect a locally rooted Democrat, though much has changed since.

## Candidates

### Brad Finstad (R)

Finstad, 50, is a fourth-generation farmer from the New Ulm area. He served in the state House from 2003 to 2008, worked in agricultural organizations, and was Minnesota director of USDA Rural Development in the first Trump administration. He won the August 2022 special election after Hagedorn died. His priorities are agriculture, rural development, federal spending, taxes and immigration enforcement.

He backs the administration's tariffs as a negotiating tool: in the October 8 debate he argued they brought trading partners to the table and pointed to exports of ethanol, dairy and soybean oil. He also promotes the 2025 Republican tax law as helping families, businesses and farmers.

### Jake Johnson (DFL)

Johnson has taught high-school math in Rochester for about 20 years. He grew up in Stewartville, one of 11 children in a working-class family (his father collected garbage, his mother read utility meters), attended UW–La Crosse, and has worked in financial-literacy education with the Minnesota Jump$tart Coalition. He won the August 11 DFL primary over Alex Eaton with about 81%.

His platform: lower household costs, protecting healthcare programs, stronger rural schools and hospitals, a bipartisan farm bill and limits on corporate influence in politics. He is critical of tariffs, the Iran war and healthcare spending cuts, and names the teacher-turned-senator Paul Wellstone as a political influence.

**The contrast.** A farmer rooted in agricultural organizations and rural Republican politics against a teacher rooted in public education and Minnesota's DFL tradition. Neither is mainly a national-politics figure.

The bigger story is outside spending: about $9.67 million in independent expenditures through October 30 (about $9.66M in the general election), roughly **$6.0M** supporting Johnson or opposing Finstad and **$3.6M** supporting Finstad or opposing Johnson. The largest spenders include Your Community PAC, House Majority PAC, No Going Back PAC and Thank You for Your Attention to This Matter PAC. The Star Tribune reported on October 30 that millions arrived in the final weeks, including a large House Majority PAC TV buy. Outside spending isn't candidate money and shouldn't be added to receipts.

## The issues

**Tariffs and agriculture.** Farmers and processors depend on export markets for soybeans, corn, pork, dairy and processed foods. Finstad calls tariffs leverage that improves deals and opens exports. Johnson says they raise costs for consumers and producers and pledges to vote to end them. They sparred over it directly at their October 8 debate in North Mankato.

**Healthcare and rural hospitals.** Mayo anchors Rochester, while smaller towns depend on rural hospitals, clinics and nursing facilities. Johnson attacks the 2025 Republican budget law's healthcare cuts and stresses protecting Medicaid and rural hospitals. Finstad emphasizes its tax provisions and Republican approaches to healthcare spending and eligibility.

**The Iran war.** Johnson criticizes the war and its costs to federal spending, military personnel and energy prices, linking it to household fuel costs. Finstad defends the administration's national-security approach. It has come up repeatedly in debates.

**Education and rural communities.** Johnson's teaching career is central: federal support for rural schools and small-town prospects. Finstad stresses local decision-making, agricultural education and rural economic development.

**Spending and accountability.** Finstad: tax relief, restraint, fewer regulations. Johnson: limits on corporate influence and congressional stock trading, protecting social insurance and healthcare, and balancing the budget.

## Why it matters nationally

- **A Republican-leaning seat with a close race.** A 12-point presidential margin and a 17-point 2024 House win against polls showing a near tie.
- **Rural and small-town voting.** Much of the district is agricultural or small regional cities, so the result is evidence about how those voters read the 2026 environment.
- **Walz's coalition.** He held MN-1 for six terms before becoming governor, a reference point for what the district can do, though it has changed.
- **Tariffs hit home.** Trade policy has direct local economic consequences in an export-heavy agricultural region.
- **A late rush of money.** About $9.7M of outside spending in a race that drew little national attention earlier in the year.
- **House control.** With a narrow Republican majority, a flip here would count; MPR News highlighted this on October 29.

That doesn't mean party identification has shifted equally; candidate effects, turnout expectations, the national environment and polling error all contribute.
