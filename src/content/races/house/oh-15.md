Ohio's 15th District pits Republican incumbent Mike Carey against Democrat Don Leonard, with Libertarian Brennan Barrington also on the ballot. It covers the southwestern Columbus metro area, with fast-growing suburbs, and extends into rural communities west and southwest of the city.

The story is the growing competitiveness of Columbus's suburbs. Trump carried the district by about 10 points in 2024, but national Democrats have invested in challenging Carey, and Cook moved the race from Likely to Lean Republican on October 16.

## The district

OH-15 includes western, southern and northern Columbus suburbs, including Dublin, Hilliard and Grove City, and rural parts of Madison, Fayette and Clinton counties.

| Area | Character |
| --- | --- |
| Dublin and Hilliard | Growing suburbs; professional jobs, technology, healthcare |
| Grove City and southwest Columbus | Residential growth, logistics, transportation, manufacturing |
| Madison and Fayette counties | Agriculture, small communities, industrial jobs |
| Clinton County | Rural; Wilmington-area logistics and manufacturing |

It sits where continued Columbus growth meets the Republican voting of many rural counties. Ohio adopted new lines in October 2025, but unlike the dramatic redraws in Texas and Florida, OH-15's presidential partisanship barely changed.

## Candidates

### Mike Carey (R)

Carey, 55, won a November 2021 special election. He worked extensively in coal, including government-affairs roles at Murray Energy and leading the Ohio Coal Association, and sits on Ways and Means. His priorities are domestic energy, manufacturing and logistics jobs, tax cuts and the Republican economic agenda; Trump endorsed his reelection in May. He voted for the 2025 Republican budget and tax law, which extended tax provisions while changing Medicaid and SNAP funding and eligibility, and describes it as tax relief for workers, families, seniors and small businesses.

### Don Leonard (D)

Leonard previously taught regional and city planning at Ohio State. He won the May primary with about 53% over former state Rep. Adam Miller. He campaigns on wages, affordability, healthcare access and sharing the benefits of growth, and criticizes Carey's Medicaid and SNAP votes, citing his own childhood experience with Medicaid.

One controversy: Leonard was arrested at a March "No Kings" protest and charged with obstructing official business, as reported by the Ohio Capital Journal. A charge isn't a conviction, and the case's final disposition isn't established here.

### Brennan Barrington (L)

Barrington wants limited federal involvement in markets, less regulation, and skepticism of tariffs and overseas military commitments. He supports cutting Medicaid and SNAP for adults while keeping aid for children, and opposes federal special-education mandates under IDEA, arguing they belong to the states. Surveys have shown only modest Libertarian support.

## Issues

**Healthcare, Medicaid and food aid.** The sharpest dispute is the 2025 budget law. Carey backs its tax provisions and spending priorities; Leonard opposes the Medicaid and SNAP changes as threats to healthcare and household security. The Ohio Capital Journal reported August protests outside Carey's Columbus office, including parents worried about Medicaid-funded services and education support.

**Columbus-area growth.** Expansion raises questions about transportation, housing costs, infrastructure and jobs. Carey emphasizes manufacturing, energy and business investment; Leonard asks whether job growth means enough wages and affordable living.

**Energy and data centers.** Central Ohio's data-center build-out raises electricity demand, grid and cost-allocation questions. Carey's energy background and tax-policy role make energy and industrial investment central to him; Leonard brings a planning perspective.

**Tariffs and household costs.** Trade affects manufacturing, farms, transportation and consumers. Tariffs, inflation, tax cuts and social-program changes are prominent across competitive Ohio districts, though statewide reporting can't show how much they matter to OH-15 voters.

## Why it matters

**Suburban change without a redraw.** The presidential baseline moved less than a point under the new map, so the interest lies in suburban voting and the national environment, not partisan engineering.

**A wider battlefield.** Cook's October 16 changes included several previously less competitive Republican seats, and OH-15 is one.
