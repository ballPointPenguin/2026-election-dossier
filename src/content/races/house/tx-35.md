Texas's 35th District is one of the clearest cases of how the state's 2025 mid-decade redistricting reshaped the 2026 House map. The old TX-35 was a safe Democratic seat linking parts of Austin and San Antonio. The new one is centered on San Antonio and reaches into more Republican territory, and its Democratic incumbent, Greg Casar, is running in TX-37 instead.

That leaves an open seat between Republican Carlos De La Cruz, an Air Force veteran and gym owner, and Democrat Johnny Garcia, a Bexar County sheriff's deputy. Trump would have won the new district by about 10 points in 2024, but the one district poll and the forecasters see a much tighter contest than that baseline suggests.

## The district

The 2025 map removed Austin from TX-35 and rebuilt it around San Antonio and territory to its east, which is a very different electorate from the one that kept electing Democrats. It includes San Antonio-area suburbs and working-class neighborhoods, Hispanic communities and military connections, plus the ties between a growing metro and smaller surrounding towns.

The right comparison isn't Casar's old margins, which came under different boundaries. On the 2026 lines Trump +10 in 2024 is a reconstructed figure, not an actual result.

It pairs naturally with [TX-15](../tx-15/): both reflect Republican efforts to consolidate South Texas and San Antonio-area support, with different candidates and electorates.

## Candidates

### Carlos De La Cruz (R)

An Air Force veteran who served overseas and at Lackland Air Force Base, De La Cruz owns a San Antonio gym and has never held elected office. He beat state Rep. John Lujan in the May runoff by about 15 points, with Trump's endorsement a big factor. He stresses economic growth, lower costs, border security, military service and fighting government corruption. He is the brother of Monica De La Cruz, the Republican in TX-15, so both siblings are on the ballot in competitive races.

### Johnny Garcia (D)

A deputy from San Antonio's Westside with nearly two decades in law enforcement, Garcia calls himself an old-school moderate Democrat and has the backing of the Blue Dog Coalition. He won the May runoff over educator Maureen Galindo by about 28 points. He runs on affordability, wages, healthcare, public safety and the economic effects of federal policy, and criticizes his own party on immigration and some social issues.

**The matchup.** Two Latino candidates from military and law-enforcement backgrounds, neither a simple progressive-versus-conservative type. The Texas Tribune explored the contrast in an October 6 profile.

## The October 8 forum

At a San Antonio Hispanic Chamber of Commerce forum moderated by Texas Public Radio, both called affordability voters' central concern, and also took on immigration, tariffs, business costs and the federal economic role.

| Issue | De La Cruz (R) | Garcia (D) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost of living | Lower taxes, business growth, less government burden | Wages, everyday expenses, household security |
| Immigration | Stronger border enforcement; Trump's broader agenda | Border security, distinguished from some Democratic positions |
| Tariffs and business | Domestic competitiveness and growth | Worried tariffs raise costs for households and small business |
| Public safety | Military experience, national security | Nearly 20 years in law enforcement |
| Party independence | Willing to differ with Republican leaders on some issues | Openly Blue Dog moderate |

Both are trying to show some distance from their national parties, which suits a district that doesn't fit national partisan stereotypes.

## Why it matters nationally

**Texas's 2025 redistricting.** A district once held by a progressive Democrat became an open seat with a much more Republican presidential history. The result adds evidence on how the new maps interact with the 2026 environment, though one election can't isolate the map's effect.

**Latino voting in Texas.** Trump gained with Latino voters in Texas in 2024, while recent statewide polling suggests preferences still respond to the economy and candidates. The Tribune describes both campaigns making direct appeals to Latino voters, with bilingual outreach and affordability messages, though neither relies on identity alone: both stress occupation, economic policy and ties to San Antonio.

## What to watch

Whether new district polling appears, turnout across San Antonio neighborhoods and surrounding communities, and whether outside groups keep adding ad money. Texas early voting runs October 19–30, so the final stretch overlaps with actual voting.
