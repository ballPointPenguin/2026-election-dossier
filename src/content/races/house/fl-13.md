Florida's 13th District, centered on Pinellas County in the Tampa Bay region, is another Republican-held seat drawing late attention. Incumbent Anna Paulina Luna faces Democrat Leela Gray, with independent Tony D'Arrigo also on the ballot. Trump won the old district by about 13 points in 2024 and Luna won by about 10 (54.8%), but Cook and Inside Elections have both moved the race from Likely to Lean Republican.

| --- | --- | --- |

| Leela Gray | Democrat | Retired Army brigadier general; first-time candidate |
| Tony D'Arrigo | Independent | From Largo; little press coverage |

## The district

FL-13 sits on the Pinellas peninsula between Tampa Bay and the Gulf of Mexico, taking in St. Petersburg, Clearwater, Largo and surrounding suburbs. Very different from Miami's FL-27: retirees, service workers, professionals, longtime residents and newcomers drawn by the coast.

Local issues:

- **Housing and insurance:** homeowners' and flood insurance premiums, condo costs, affordability.
- **Hurricanes:** coastal flooding, storm recovery, resilience, federal disaster aid.
- **Healthcare and retirement:** Medicare, Social Security, healthcare costs.
- **Economy:** tourism, small business, wages, cost of living.

**New map.** Florida enacted a new congressional map in May 2026 that changed FL-13's southern boundary and shuffled parts of Pinellas between districts. Per the Pinellas Supervisor of Elections, 127,406 voters moved from the old FL-13 to the new FL-16, and 74,710 moved from the old FL-14 into FL-13. These are district reassignments, not moves. So Luna's 54.8% and Trump's +13 describe the old lines, not the electorate voting this November. The [Florida Legislature's 2026 maps](https://www.edr.state.fl.us/content/redistricting/2026redistricting/congressional.cfm) have the precise geography.

## Candidates

### Anna Paulina Luna (R)

Luna, an Air Force veteran and former political commentator, first won the seat in 2022, succeeding Democrat Charlie Crist. She is a prominent Trump supporter with an outsized national profile for a junior member, built on oversight, investigations, immigration and government transparency. Her campaign stresses border enforcement, conservative fiscal policy and her legislative record. The flip side of that profile is that voters can read the race as a referendum on the administration.

### Leela Gray (D)

Gray is a retired Army brigadier general with more than three decades of service and degrees in journalism, strategic studies and law (J.D., Stetson). She won the August primary with about 65% over Brandt Robinson and John Liccione. Her campaign emphasizes affordability, government accountability, homeowners' insurance, healthcare and economic opportunity. October ads highlight her working-class roots: her father a mechanic, her family relying on food assistance.

### Tony D'Arrigo (I)

D'Arrigo qualified for the ballot with no party affiliation. He polled 7% in the late-August three-way poll and 4% in an earlier GQR survey, though poll support is not election-day support.

**A dispute.** In September, Luna's campaign challenged a Gray ad alleging corruption and personal financial benefit. Gray defended the ad and called the challenge an effort to dodge scrutiny. The charges are contested.

## Why it matters nationally

**Two veterans.** Both candidates have military backgrounds, though very different ones: Luna in the Air Force before turning commentator and politician, Gray 30-plus years in the Army. Military service doesn't sort cleanly by party here; each leans on a different kind of public-service credential.

**National politics versus local affordability.** Luna has a national Trump-aligned profile; Gray talks household costs and insurance premiums. Both campaigns mix national and local themes, but their emphases differ.

**Coastal Florida versus Miami.** FL-13 and FL-27 are both Florida Republican seats that went for Trump in 2024 and are drawing more Democratic attention, but their electorates and issues differ:

## What to watch

How voters moved in and out of the district behave compared with 2024, whether August's numbers hold and how much D'Arrigo draws, how spending shapes the final stretch, and turnout among Republicans, Democrats and unaffiliated voters versus the presidential-year electorate.
