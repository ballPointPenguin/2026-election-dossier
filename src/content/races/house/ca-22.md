California's 22nd District is a Republican-held seat in the southern San Joaquin Valley, covering farm communities and parts of Bakersfield. Two things stand out: Republican David Valadao has repeatedly won in Democratic-leaning territory, and California's new congressional map substantially changed the district for 2026.

Valadao, a dairy farmer and longtime congressman, faces Randy Villegas, a Democratic college professor and Visalia school board member.

| Candidate | Party | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| David Valadao | Republican | Dairy farmer from Hanford; in Congress since 2013 (lost in 2018, back in 2020); Appropriations |
| Randy Villegas | Democrat | Political science professor, College of the Sequoias; Visalia Unified school board; progressive |

## The district

The new CA-22 includes portions of Kings, Tulare, Fresno and Kern counties: the Hanford area, farmland around Visalia and eastern Bakersfield.

| Region or industry | Significance |
| --- | --- |
| Agriculture and dairies | Dairy, orchards, field crops, irrigation, farm labor |
| Bakersfield and Kern County | Oil and gas, manufacturing, logistics, healthcare |
| Kings and Tulare counties | Agriculture, food processing, small cities and rural communities |
| Water | Groundwater limits, irrigation deliveries, drought, competing demands |
| Healthcare and education | Rural hospitals, specialist access, schools, workforce development |

A large Latino population and heavy agricultural employment make immigration and farm labor especially relevant, alongside cost of living and water.

**The redistricting change.** The seat is one of those affected by Proposition 50, approved by voters in November 2025, which temporarily replaced the independently drawn districts with a legislative map for the 2026–2030 elections amid a national fight over mid-decade redistricting. The 2026 CA-22 isn't the district Valadao held in 2024: it added Democratic-friendlier territory in parts of Fresno and eastern Bakersfield.

Inside Elections puts the new baseline at about D+7.7, while Cook's PVI is D+1. They measure different things (Inside's estimates a two-party voting environment, Cook's compares presidential performance with the nation), so they aren't conflicting readings of the same quantity. Valadao's earlier results show his history but can't just be carried forward.

## Candidates

### David Valadao (R)

He has served on Appropriations, including its agriculture and defense subcommittees, with interests in water infrastructure, agriculture, veterans and federal spending. His record is unusual for a Republican in a competitive seat: he was one of ten House Republicans who voted to impeach Trump after January 6, 2021, and per CalMatters is the last still in Congress who did; he has repeatedly won districts with Democratic-leaning presidential histories; and he stresses working across the aisle on agriculture and local infrastructure.

### Randy Villegas (D)

A progressive who stresses healthcare, education, affordability, labor rights and opportunity for Central Valley families. He won the nomination by beating better-established Assemblymember Jasmeet Bains, who had national Democratic backing.

## The issues

**Healthcare and Medicaid.** Valadao's vote for the 2025 Republican tax and spending law is central. Democrats say its Medicaid provisions threaten coverage and rural-hospital finances, and Villegas stresses this in communities with many lower-income and farmworker families; Valadao and Republican backers stress its tax provisions and argue for changes to healthcare spending and eligibility. The law changes federal Medicaid spending and eligibility over time, and actual effects depend on implementation and state responses.

**Immigration and farm labor.** The Central Valley relies on immigrant labor in farming and processing, so enforcement affects employers, workers, families and local economies. Valadao has historically backed some immigration reform and legal status for certain undocumented immigrants alongside enforcement; Villegas stresses immigrant protections, workers' rights and criticism of aggressive enforcement.

**Water.** Farms depend on groundwater, irrigation systems and surface deliveries, with drought, groundwater depletion and state requirements competing among agriculture, communities and ecosystems. Valadao has worked on deliveries and infrastructure; Villegas stresses workers and communities alongside sustainability. Federal and state agencies, irrigation districts and local stakeholders drive the policy, so neither candidate controls it alone.

**Oil, gas and environment.** Kern County is a major oil region and eastern Bakersfield is affected by energy jobs and environmental conditions. The candidates differ on fossil-fuel development, regulation and climate policy, tied to energy prices, air quality and the state's shift to lower-carbon energy.

## Why it matters nationally

- **A test of Proposition 50.** The map was meant to shift the partisan balance of several districts, and CA-22 is among the most affected, within a national fight over mid-decade redistricting.
- **An unusual incumbent.** The impeachment vote and repeated wins in Democratic-leaning ground set Valadao apart.
- **A progressive nominee.** Villegas's win over an establishment-backed legislator tests how Democratic constituencies show up in a general election.
- **Agriculture at the center.** Water, immigrant labor, healthcare and food production shape jobs and daily life.
- **2024 isn't the 2026 baseline.** Valadao beat Democrat Rudy Salas in 2024 by 53.4% to 46.6% (11,461 votes), but under the previous map.
- **California's wider contest.** One of several Republican-held seats redrawn under Proposition 50, alongside districts like [CA-48](../ca-48/).
