Florida's 22nd District is one of the more unusual contests of 2026. A formerly Democratic-leaning Palm Beach County district was redrawn into a sprawling cross-peninsula seat, producing an open-seat race between two people who have never served in Congress: Democrat Pia Dandiya, with a technology background, and Republican Casey Askar, a businessman and Marine veteran.

The old district voted for Harris in 2024; Trump would have carried the new one by about 10 points. Limited public polling nonetheless shows a close race, and national analysts are paying close attention.

## The district

FL-22 is no longer a Palm Beach coastal district. The May 2026 map joins parts of Palm Beach and Broward counties with inland Hendry County and parts of Collier County, reaching toward Marco Island on the Gulf Coast. CBS Miami described it on October 11 as built from pieces of at least four previous districts.

- **Atlantic side:** urban and suburban Palm Beach and Broward, with healthcare, professional services, tourism and retirees.
- **Interior:** agricultural communities tied to sugar and farming, plus Everglades territory.
- **Southwest Florida:** Collier County, with tourism, real estate, construction and retirement services.

It crosses several media markets, which makes reaching voters a logistical challenge.

## Candidates

### Pia Dandiya (D)

A former teacher and school principal who has also worked in technology, Dandiya won the August 18 primary with about 68.6% over attorney Kaysia Earley. She runs on affordability, housing and insurance costs, prescription drugs, Social Security and healthcare, and ethics reform. In an October 16 WGCU interview she named ending tariffs and restoring ACA subsidies as early priorities.

### Casey Askar (R)

A Naples-area businessman and Marine veteran who ran for Congress in 2020, Askar won a seven-candidate primary with about 30.9%. He campaigns on tax cuts, deregulation, border security, Trump's agenda and changes to federal healthcare policy. He wants to replace the ACA while preserving Medicare and Medicaid through spending reforms.

## The policy differences

| Issue | Dandiya (D) | Askar (R) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Household costs | Lower housing, insurance, childcare and drug costs | Lower taxes, less regulation |
| Healthcare | Restore ACA subsidies; protect coverage | Replace the ACA; change federal healthcare spending |
| Trade | End tariffs she says raise prices | Lower business costs; Trump's economic agenda |
| Accountability | Congressional stock-trading limits; lobbying reform | Cut government waste; election integrity |
| Immigration | Economic and community impacts alongside enforcement | Stronger border enforcement; backs administration policy |

Based on platforms and interviews, not voting records. Two issues stand out for this geography:

- **Healthcare and retirement security.** Large older populations make Medicare, Social Security and drug costs especially salient.
- **Housing, insurance and resilience.** Atlantic and Gulf coasts plus the inland farm and Everglades areas bring overlapping worries about insurance, flooding, hurricanes, water management and development.

## Why it matters nationally

**Redistricting.** The map turned a seat that voted Democratic for president in 2024 into one Trump would have won by double digits, with no incumbent in the way.

**Disconnected communities.** Palm Beach and Broward suburbs, the Everglades farm interior and the Naples–Marco Island area don't form a conventional metro district, raising practical questions about representation and campaigning.

**Candidate backgrounds.** Education and technology against military service and business ownership. Neither has a congressional record, so voters are judging earlier careers and proposals.

**House control.** The DCCC and NRCC have both targeted FL-22. Compare Florida's other redrawn contests: [FL-13](../fl-13/), [FL-14](../fl-14/) and [FL-27](../fl-27/).

## What to watch

Whether new October polling appears, turnout on the Atlantic versus Gulf sides, whether the campaigns' very different funding structures affect final-week advertising, and how voters take the affordability and healthcare messages.
